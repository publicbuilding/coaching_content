### Issue 2: The 90-Day Framework

```markdown
# The 90-Day Framework That Got Me the Job

When I interviewed for my current role, my boss asked, "What's your first 90 days look like?"

I said something like, "I'll learn the team, build relationships, understand strategy." Vague. Everyone says that.

But in my head, I had a framework. I'd learned it the hard way through two failed promotions, a lot of embarrassing meetings, and finally sitting down to reverse-engineer what worked for executives I respected.

It's simple enough to fit on a napkin, detailed enough to execute.

---

## The Framework

**Days 1-30: Listen**
- 1-on-1s with every direct report (60 min each)
- 1-on-1s with peer leaders (30 min each)
- Stakeholder mapping (who needs what from you?)
- Read the last 6 months of Slack/emails (really)
- Audit existing processes (document what's working, what's not)
- **Goal:** Understand the system you've just entered

**Days 31-60: Diagnose**
- Identify the 3-5 biggest problems (not everything, just the critical ones)
- Build support for fixing them (yes, this takes politicking)
- Plan first wins (small changes that prove you listen)
- Prioritize ruthlessly (drop everything that isn't a win or foundational)
- **Goal:** Show leadership that you understand the problem

**Days 61-90: Move**
- Execute first wins (visible wins = credibility)
- Build on early wins (people start believing you)
- Plan 90-180 roadmap (what's next after you stabilize)
- Demonstrate impact (numbers, not feelings)
- **Goal:** Have something real to show on day 91

---

## Why This Works

The classic mistake: new leaders come in and immediately try to change everything. They think they're being decisive. Actually, they're being reckless. You don't have enough context to make good decisions yet.

The framework forces you to **listen first**. Not waste the credibility you walked in with.

---

## Real Example

I used this at my last job. On day 5, I was deep in meetings. On day 15, I noticed that the same issue came up 7 times: we were slow on customer decisions because the approval chain was broken. On day 25, I had evidence.

On day 35, I proposed a fix. Not a massive restructure, just: "Let's move this decision down to this team." It saved 2 weeks per cycle. By day 60, the CEO had noticed. It was a small win, but it positioned me as someone who listened and fixed things.

That framework one change unlocked bigger projects later.
```

### Issue 3: The One Thing New Executives Get Wrong

```markdown
# The One Thing Most New Executives Get Wrong (And Why You'll Probably Do It Too)

Here's the pattern I see:

Month 1: New executive is humble. Learning. Asking questions. Everyone thinks they're great.

Month 2: New executive has seen enough. They think they understand. They start making changes. Some of the changes are good. Some are not.

Month 3: New executive realizes they moved too fast. They made an enemy of someone they didn't think would matter. They broke something they didn't know was important.

The damage is done.

---

## The Mistake

**They mistake speed for leadership.**

They think: "I need to show impact fast. Make bold moves. Prove myself."

Reality: Your team is watching to see if you're safe. Will you listen? Will you blow up existing relationships? Will you treat them like humans or just resources?

Move slowly in month 2 and 3 and you have credit in the bank. Move fast and you burn it.

---

## The Pattern (What Actually Happens)

You announce a reorganization that "fixes efficiency." You didn't realize the current structure was a political compromise. You just broke the peace.

You change the decision-making process. People now have to ask your permission for things they used to own. You've just told them you don't trust them.

You hire an outsider for a role a long-time team member wanted. That person was loyal. Now they're looking for another job.

You're trying to lead. They're seeing you as a threat.

---

## How to Actually Move (The Right Way)

**Month 1 action:** Get consensus on what's broken.
- In your 1-on-1s, listen for the patterns.
- "What's frustrating you most right now?" They'll tell you.
- When the same problem comes up 5 times, you've found it.
- **Don't suggest solutions yet.** Just listen.

**Month 2 action:** Get buy-in on the fix.
- Bring together the people affected. "Here's what I'm hearing. Is this right?"
- Let them help design the solution. They'll spot what you missed.
- Build consensus before you announce. Not unanimous agreement—but enough people say "yeah, that makes sense."
- **Now** you move.

**Month 3 action:** Execute and iterate.
- The plan won't be perfect. It's okay. You built consensus.
- When things break, people help you fix it because they helped design it.
- You've gone from "new leader threatening our stability" to "leader who listened and delivered."

---

## The Real Cost

Speed costs you relationships. Relationships cost you everything else.

If your team trusts you, they forgive you when you mess up. They cover for you. They give you hard feedback instead of just leaving.

If they don't trust you, every decision you make is questioned. Every change is threatened. You spend energy fighting people who should be helping you.

---

## Bottom Line

Leadership isn't moving fast. It's moving smart. Move slow when you don't have context. Move fast when you do.

You have 90 days to build context. Use them.
```

## ⚠️ Common Mistakes

❌ **Too many issues too fast.** You'll burn out. Stick to 1x/week.

❌ **Asking for paid tier too early.** Build trust first (free tier). Launch paid tier in week 2-3.

❌ **Treating it like a product dump.** This is not a marketing channel for Gumroad. It's a relationship. Sell second.

❌ **Not following up on replies.** People reply to your issues. Respond to EVERY one in first month. It builds community.

✅ **Consistency.** Same day, same time, every week. People plan for it.

✅ **Personality.** Be real. Stories beat frameworks.

✅ **CTA clarity.** End with a call-to-action (reply, upgrade, check the store).
