# The #1 Negotiation Mistake Smart People Make

## Target Audience
- Executives negotiating deals, contracts, partnerships
- Business owners in high-stakes negotiations
- Leaders wanting better negotiation outcomes

## Document Length
- **Target:** 4-5 pages
- **Read Time:** 10 minutes

---

## I. The Mistake (What It Looks Like)
**Length:** 0.5 page | **Time:** 1 minute

### The Mistake Itself
**Making your opening offer first.**

### Why Smart People Do This
- Think they're being reasonable and transparent
- Believe first offer anchors the negotiation
- Want to "get the ball rolling"
- Think it builds goodwill
- Afraid of looking unprepared if asked

### Why It's Deadly
The person who makes the opening offer almost always loses.

### The Proof
Research by Harvard negotiation expert shows:
- **70% of negotiations** end within 15% of first offer made
- First offer setter pays **$50K-$200K more** in typical business deals
- Effect size: **Much larger** than most people realize

---

## II. The Psychology: Why First Offer Wins
**Length:** 1 page | **Time:** 2 minutes

### Anchoring Effect
**What it is:** First number mentioned becomes the reference point for entire negotiation.

**How it works:**
1. You offer: "$500K for acquisition"
2. Other person's brain: "Okay, baseline is $500K"
3. Everything negotiated against that anchor
4. Even if they counter at $350K, they started from your $500K baseline

### The Math of Anchoring
```
Example 1: You make offer first
Your offer: $500K
Their counter: $350K (30% discount)
Typical settlement: ~$425K (halfway point)
You expected: $500K, got $425K (loss of $75K)

Example 2: They make offer first
Their offer: $250K
Your counter: $400K
Typical settlement: ~$325K (halfway point)
You expected: $250K, got $325K (gain of $75K)
Plus, $150K difference in outcome!
```

### Why Anchoring Works (Even When You Know About It)
- It's **subconscious** (you can't turn it off by knowing)
- It affects everyone (**not just naive people**)
- Works even when the anchor is **obviously wrong**
- Works in salary negotiations, legal settlements, M&A, partnerships

### Real Example: Salary Negotiation
- You start: "I'm looking for $150K"
- Recruiter hears: "Baseline is $150K"
- Recruiter counter: "We can do $130K"
- Typical outcome: ~$140K
- **If they'd offered first:** "We offer $120K" → typical outcome $135K → you lose $5K minimum

## III. The Power Move: Get Them to Offer First
**Length:** 1 page | **Time:** 2 minutes

### How to Do It

#### Tactic 1: Directly Ask for Their Number
**What to say:**
- "What did you have in mind?"
- "What's your budget for this?"
- "What were you thinking?"
- "What does success look like price-wise for you?"

**Why it works:**
- Direct, non-threatening
- Gives them burden of anchoring themselves
- If they refuse, you're no worse off

#### Tactic 2: The Hypothetical
**What to say:**
- "If we could solve X, what would you be comfortable investing?"
- "In an ideal scenario, what would this be worth to you?"
- "Help me understand the parameters. What's the realistic range?"

**Why it works:**
- Seems collaborative
- Takes pressure off them
- Frames it as exploration, not hardball

#### Tactic 3: Show Uncertainty
**What to say:**
- "I'm not sure what the right number is here. What's your thinking?"
- "This is our first negotiation like this. What's typical?"
- "Help me understand the market. What are others paying?"

**Why it works:**
- Shows respect for their judgment
- Removes ego (you're asking for help)
- They feel intelligent and in control
- People are more likely to go first if asked

#### Tactic 4: Deflect and Redirect
**When they ask:** "What's your price?"

**Don't say:** "We're looking for $500K"
**Say instead:** "Before I give a number, help me understand what you're envisioning. How do you see this working?"

**Why it works:**
- Postpones your offer
- Learns their thinking first
- Often you realize their offer is better than what you were planning

#### Tactic 5: The Breakdown Approach
**When they ask:** "What's your price?"

**Say instead:** "Let's break down the components:
- Service value: $50K
- Implementation: $30K
- Training: $15K
- Support: $20K
- **Total: $115K**

What are your thoughts on those components?"

**Why it works:**
- Shows you've thought it through
- Invites their input on pieces (not total)
- Often they negotiate components, not total price
- You control the framework

## IV. What If They Won't Go First?
**Length:** 0.5 pages | **Time:** 1 minute

### When They Say: "You go first, I want to know what you're asking"

**Option A: Use Breakdown (above)**
- Shows you've thought it through
- Gives them role in discussion
- Often avoids need for them to state number

**Option B: Give a Range**
- "We typically see this run $X to $Y"
- "Market range is $A to $B"
- **They'll usually pick your low number or go lower**
- **Better than a point estimate, but not as good as them going first**

**Option C: Ask More Questions**
- "Before I quote, help me understand..."
- "Tell me more about..."
- Keep investigating until they reveal their budget/needs
- Often they slip and tell you their max

**Option D: If Forced to Quote**
- Quote high (you'll get negotiated down)
- Give a **range** with low on low side (they'll anchor to your high number)
- Example: "We're looking at $250K-$350K depending on scope"
- **Most will assume you meant $350K and negotiate down from there**

## V. Advanced Tactics (When Stakes Are High)
**Length:** 0.5 pages | **Time:** 1 minute

### Tactic: The Silent Treatment
**When they ask:** "What's your number?"

**Just wait.** Don't say anything.
- Awkward silence is powerful
- Other person will get uncomfortable
- Most likely to break and offer themselves
- **Psychological discomfort drives disclosure**

**How long:** Uncomfortable, but not rude. Maybe 10-15 seconds feels like 60.

### Tactic: The Justification Flip
**When they ask:** "What's your price?"

**Say:** "Before we get there, what's your absolute walk-away number? So we don't waste time on offers you can't accept."

**Why it works:**
- Seems reasonable (don't waste time)
- Gets them to reveal their floor
- Often reveals budget they wouldn't have mentioned

### Tactic: The Comparative
**When they ask:** "What's your price?"

**Say:** "In the market, we typically see $X for similar scope. What are you comparing us to?"

**Why it works:**
- Positions you as data-driven
- Often reveals they have other quotes
- Gets info on your competition
- Creates opportunity to differentiate

## VI. What You Learn When They Go First
**Length:** 0.5 pages | **Time:** 1 minute

### Red Flags (They're Offering Too Much)
- Offer is higher than you expected
- Offer is 20%+ above market
- They seem eager/almost relieved

**What to do:** Don't immediately accept. Ask more questions.
- "Help me understand how you arrived at this number"
- "What's driving that investment level?"
- Often reveals budget was larger than necessary

### Green Flags (Fair Offer)
- Offer is close to market rate
- Justification makes sense
- They seem thoughtful but firm

**What to do:** Negotiate normally from here.

### Orange Flags (They're Offering Too Little)
- Offer is below market
- Offer seems disrespectfully low
- Offer doesn't account for scope

**What to do:** Don't reject emotionally. Educate:
- "Here's why that's below market..."
- "Here's what that price would mean for scope..."
- Often they'll improve offer without you asking

## VII. The Complete Negotiation Sequence (When You Refuse to Go First)
**Length:** 0.5 pages | **Time:** 1 minute

### Step 1: Define the Problem
*You:* "Help me understand what you're trying to achieve with this..."

### Step 2: Ask About Budget/Parameters
*You:* "What's your thinking on investment for this?"

### Step 3: Get Them to Move First
*You:* "What do you see as a fair price?"

### Step 4: Respond to Their Offer
*You:* "That's interesting. Here's why we'd see it differently..."

### Step 5: Make Counter (Now from Their Anchor)
*You:* "We'd be thinking more like [number higher than their offer, but showing good faith]"

### Step 6: Negotiate From There
- Your counter is anchored to their offer (favorable to you)
- They negotiate down from their anchor (favorable to you)
- You meet somewhere between that's closer to your actual target

**Key:** They made first offer, so all negotiation is from their anchor forward.

---

## VIII. One More Thing: The Exception (When YOU Should Go First)
**Length:** 0.3 pages | **Time:** 0.5 minute

### Very Rare Cases Where Going First Helps
1. **You have 100% information** about market/fair price
2. **You're vastly more powerful** than other party (they have to accept)
3. **You want to anchor very high** on purpose (you know you'll get negotiated down)

### Usually Not Worth It
- Upside risk (they were willing to pay more)
- Downside risk (they think less of you for asking)
- **Better to let them move first, then control negotiation from there**
