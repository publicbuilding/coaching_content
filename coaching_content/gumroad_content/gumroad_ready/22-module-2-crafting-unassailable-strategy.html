Introduction: What Separates Strategists From Planners

Most executives confuse strategy with planning.

Strategy is about competitive choice: "What are we going to do that our competitors won't or can't?"

Planning is about execution: "How are we going to get it done?"

You can have excellent planning without strategy. You execute flawlessly on a mediocre plan, and you still lose to a competitor with a better strategy.

You can have brilliant strategy without planning. You know what to do, but you don't actually do it, so it doesn't matter.

The best outcomes happen when you have both. But if forced to choose, strategy beats planning every time.

Why Strategy Matters

A strategic advantage compounds over time.

Year 1: Your competitor has a better product. You're maybe 20% less desirable.

Year 2: Your competitor has built more revenue, so they invest more in R&D, pulling further ahead. Year 3: Your competitor's larger team and budget create even bigger advantage.

By Year 5, there's no path back. The gap is too big.

But if you have strategic advantage—you've positioned yourself in a market segment where your strengths matter and their strengths don't—the math reverses.

You don't need a bigger budget. You need to be playing a different game.

What This Module Teaches

This is not Porter's Five Forces. You probably know Porter. Everyone does. And Porter is useful for analyzing industries. But he doesn't tell you what to do about it.

This module teaches:

2. How to build defensibility — moats that compound over time and get harder for competitors to cross 3. Positioning — the often-overlooked source of strategic advantage 4. Execution as strategy — why most strategies fail (not the strategy, but the execution) 5. Risk mitigation — because perfect strategy fails when you're unprepared for downside scenarios

By the end, you'll understand:

  • How to build advantage in a market where everyone has the same information
  • How to position in a way that makes competition irrelevant (rather than winning the same competition everyone else is in)
  • How to execute a strategy without it dying from a thousand cuts

Who This Is For

  • Operating executives responsible for a business unit's competitive performance
  • Boards and investors evaluating whether management has a defensible strategy
  • Career executives who want to move into strategic roles (CEO, COO, strategic planning) and need to understand how strategy actually works

If you've ever executed perfectly on a strategy that still didn't win, this module explains why and what to do about it.

Chapter 1: The Strategic Thinking Framework

Strategy is the answer to one question: "Given our strengths and the market we're in, what's the most defensible competitive position we can achieve?"

That's it. Everything else is tactics.

The Three Dimensions of Strategy

Every sustainable competitive advantage has three components:

1. Market Position: Where We Compete

What customer segment, geography, or use case are we targeting?

This seems obvious, but most companies fail here. They try to serve everyone. They compete in markets where larger competitors have advantage. They chase every trend.

Strategic clarity starts with saying no to 90% of opportunities and yes to 10%.

2. Competitive Differentiation: Why We Win

What do we do better than alternatives?

This has to be real differentiation, not marketing spin.

Real differentiation comes from:

  • Better technology (we solve a problem they can't solve)
  • Better model (we cost less, we serve faster, we're more convenient)
  • Better brand (customers prefer us for emotional/identity reasons)
  • Better customer intimacy (we know their business better, we integrate deeper)
3. Defensibility: Why They Can't Easily Copy

What makes our advantage durable?

This is where most strategies fail. You have a competitive advantage today, but your competitor copies it tomorrow and you're back to competing on price.

Defensibility comes from:

  • Scale economies (harder to compete at our price because we're bigger)
  • Switching costs (expensive or difficult for customers to leave us)
  • Network effects (more valuable as we grow)
  • Proprietary assets (patents, data, talent, relationships)

The Strategic Positioning Matrix

Plot your strategy on three dimensions:

{{CODEBLOCK0}}

Position A: Commodity
  • Low differentiation, low defensibility
  • You're competing on price
  • Margins compress
  • Unsustainable position
Position B: Temporary Advantage
  • High differentiation, low defensibility
  • You have a better product, but it's easy to copy
  • You're ahead now, but you're being chased
  • You need moat-building or you'll lose
  • This is where most VC-funded companies start
Position C: Unassailable Position
  • High differentiation AND high defensibility
  • You have advantage and it's durable
  • Competitors can see you winning, but they can't easily replicate
  • This is the goal
Position D: Differentiated But Fragile
  • This doesn't really exist in sustainable form
  • High differentiation without defensibility collapses to commodity
The strategic goal: Move from B to C as fast as possible.

Most companies fail because they stay in B too long. They build great products, they win customers, but they don't build defensibility. Competitors copy them. They lose.

The winning move is: "Build the product that wins (get to B), then obsess on defensibility (move to C)."

The Strategic Diagnosis: Are You Actually Defensible?

Ask yourself these questions:

1. Can a well-funded competitor replicate what we do in 18-24 months?

If yes, you're not defensible. You need to build moat.

If no, you might be defensible. You need to understand why.

2. What would it take for a customer to leave us?

Cost: "If competitor is 20% cheaper, would customer switch?"

Switching cost: "Would migration cost $2M? Would it take 6 months?" Preference: "Do they genuinely prefer us, or would they switch for price?"

3. Are we becoming harder or easier to compete against?

As you grow, do you have more data, more relationships, more resources? (Getting harder to compete = defensible)

Or does your competitor catch up quickly? (Getting easier to compete = not defensible)

4. What's the barrier to entry for a new competitor?

Technical: "Do they need specific IP or expertise?"

Financial: "How much capital would they need to compete?" Customer: "How hard is it to win customers in this space?"

5. Are we in a winner-take-most or winner-take-some market?

Winner-take-most (e.g., social networks, payment networks): Network effects dominate. The leader compounds. Everyone else loses.

Winner-take-some (e.g., software, manufacturing): Multiple competitors can be profitable. You don't need to win all.

Chapter 2: The Competitive Moat - Building Defensibility

A "moat" is a durable competitive advantage. Something that makes it hard for competitors to catch you.

There are six types. Each has different implications for how you compete and how you build defensibility.

Moat 1: Scale Economics

Definition: Your cost per unit drops as you grow, so larger companies win.

How it works:

A car manufacturer with 10M units annually spreads fixed costs (R&D, facilities, tooling) over more units than a competitor with 2M units. Same price, higher margin. Or lower price, same margin.

At scale, you can outspend competitors on R&D or undercut them on price.

Examples:
  • Netflix: Scale lets them spend more on content while maintaining margins
  • Amazon: Scale in logistics means they pay less per package
  • Intel: Scale in manufacturing means lowest cost per chip
  • Walmart: Scale means lowest cost to operate stores and negotiate with suppliers
How to build this moat:

1. Get big in your core business before you diversify (focus beats breadth)

2. Use cost leadership to capture more share from competitors (lower price, capture share, get bigger, lower cost further) 3. Reinvest savings into areas competitors can't afford (better quality, better service, more R&D) The danger: This moat only works if you can get to scale faster than competitors. Once multiple players reach scale, the advantage dissipates.

If your competitor is also at scale, you're back to competing on product/service quality.

Moat 2: Switching Costs

Definition: It's expensive or difficult for customers to switch to a competitor.

How it works:

If your enterprise software is integrated into a customer's entire business, switching means:

  • Re-training employees on new software
  • Migrating data (expensive, error-prone)
  • Potential downtime or operational disruption
  • Opportunity cost of your team's time on migration vs. other priorities

At some point, the cost of switching exceeds the benefit of switching to a cheaper competitor.

Examples:
  • Salesforce: Switching means re-implementing CRM across your whole sales organization
  • Microsoft Office: Switching means retraining employees and potentially losing compatibility
  • Workday: Switching means replacing your entire financial system (massive undertaking)
How to build this moat:

1. Integrate deep into customer's business (more integration = higher switching cost)

2. Make switching costly in terms of training, data migration, or operational disruption 3. Build in lock-in through contractual commitments or long implementation timelines 4. Invest in customer success (customers who get more value from you are less likely to switch) The danger: Switching cost moats can backfire if you abuse them.

Customers who feel locked in often look for alternatives more aggressively. When they finally switch, they're gone for good.

Better to have switching costs from value, not from friction.

Moat 3: Network Effects

Definition: Your product becomes more valuable as more people use it.

How it works:

A social network with 1M users is valuable. With 100M users, it's 10x more valuable (more connections, more content, more reasons to check it).

A competitor starting from scratch has to build to scale before they're even competitive.

Examples:
  • Facebook: Network value increases with each user
  • Visa/Mastercard: More valuable with more merchants and consumers
  • LinkedIn: More valuable with more professionals
  • Slack: More valuable when your whole company is on it
How to build this moat:

1. Create network value early (even when small, show how the product gets better with scale)

2. Acquire seed users in a concentrated way (get critical mass in one segment first, then expand) 3. Make network switching costly (if all your colleagues use Slack, it's hard to switch to a new tool) 4. Subsidize early adopters (in a network effect business, early users have disproportionate value) The danger: Network effects flip if a competitor reaches critical mass faster.

Once they do, you're behind and getting further behind (more users = more value = more users).

This is why network effect businesses move fast in the early stages.

Moat 4: Brand

Definition: Customers prefer your product because of brand perception, not just objective quality.

How it works:

Two pairs of shoes with similar quality. One is Nike, one is Generic Brand. Nike commands price premium because of brand.

Customers prefer Nike for status, identity, or simply trust from experience.

Examples:
  • Nike: Brand allows premium pricing and loyalty
  • Apple: Brand commands price premium despite similar functionality
  • Coca-Cola: Brand preference despite commodity underlying product
  • Disney: Brand creates emotional connection and pricing power
How to build this moat:

1. Deliver exceptional product/service consistently (brand trust comes from reliable experience)

2. Tell a compelling story (why should customers care about you beyond function?) 3. Invest in brand awareness (take share of mind) 4. Create emotional connection (do customers identify with your brand?) 5. Be willing to say no to low-margin opportunities that damage brand The danger: Brand is fragile.

One bad experience, one scandal, one perceived betrayal and brand trust erodes quickly.

Also: Brand alone isn't defensible if the product becomes obsolete. You need underlying quality.

Moat 5: Proprietary Assets

Definition: You own something valuable that competitors can't easily replicate.

How it works:

Patents protect your technology for 20 years. Your dataset is worth billions because competitors can't replicate the data collection. Your distribution network took decades to build.

These are hard assets with real value and real defensibility.

Examples:
  • Pfizer: Patents protect drug formulations
  • Google: Data and algorithms in search (hard to replicate)
  • Coca-Cola: Distribution network and supplier relationships
  • Oracle: Patents, installed base, and ecosystem
How to build this moat:

1. Invest in R&D and protect with patents (but understand patent life is limited)

2. Build proprietary data and algorithms (harder to replicate than patents, often more durable) 3. Develop deep customer relationships and switching costs (turns relationship into asset) 4. Build distribution network that's hard to replicate (took decades to build, takes decades to beat) 5. Develop proprietary talent (hard to hire, develop, and keep) The danger: Technology patents last 20 years but technology often becomes obsolete faster.

Data can be commoditized if competitors build their own data.

---

Moat 6: Organizational Capital

Definition: You have processes, culture, or talent that competitors can't easily replicate.

How it works:

McKinsey's competitive advantage isn't IP or patents. It's culture, brand, and a training process that turns smart people into exceptional consultants.

Toyota's competitive advantage is manufacturing processes and continuous improvement culture that took decades to build.

Examples:
  • McKinsey: Culture, people, and methodology
  • Toyota: Manufacturing processes and continuous improvement (kaizen)
  • Southwest Airlines: Culture and operational efficiency
  • Amazon: Data-driven decision culture and logistics excellence
How to build this moat:

1. Develop distinctive processes (not in your head, in your systems)

2. Build talent advantage (hire better, develop better, retain better) 3. Create culture that competitors can't easily replicate (culture is sticky) 4. Document and systematize knowledge (reduces dependence on key people) 5. Build reputation as great place to work (virtuous cycle: great talent → great results → attracts more talent) The danger: Organizational capital is most fragile to executive turnover.

If your star CEO leaves, your competitive advantage often leaves with them.

Combining Moats: The Unassailable Position

The strongest positions have multiple moats.

Amazon:

  • Scale economics (largest e-commerce, logistics network)
  • Switching costs (integration with AWS, Prime benefits)
  • Network effects (marketplace becomes more valuable)
  • Data (understanding of customer behavior)
  • Organizational capital (culture of efficiency and innovation)

Disrupting Amazon requires beating them on multiple dimensions. You can't win on just product or price.

Apple:

  • Brand (emotional connection to Apple ecosystem)
  • Proprietary assets (chips, designs)
  • Switching costs (ecosystem lock-in)
  • Organizational capital (design and manufacturing culture)
  • Scale (resources to invest in R&D)

Note: Apple is not competing on price or cost leadership. They're competing on brand, design, and ecosystem value. That's a different game than competitors like Samsung or Microsoft.

The strategic insight: Figure out which moats are available to you, and build multiple ones.

You don't have the scale of Amazon (probably). Can you build brand, switching costs, and proprietary assets?

You don't have the brand of Apple. Can you build scale, switching costs, and data advantages?

Chapter 3: Positioning - The Real Source of Advantage

Moats protect your position. But before you build moats, you need to choose a position.

This is where most companies fail.

They try to be all things to all people. They compete in markets where larger players have advantage. They chase trends.

Great strategy starts with choosing a position where you can win.

The Positioning Decision: Market Segment

Choose your customer and use case first. Everything else follows from that choice.

Dimensions of positioning:

1. Customer type:

- Fortune 500 enterprises vs. mid-market vs. SMBs vs. individual consumers - Specific industry (healthcare, financial services, manufacturing) - Stage (startups vs. scale-ups vs. enterprises)

2. Use case / Problem:

- Core problem your customer solves - Severity of the problem (is it critical or nice-to-have?) - Willingness to pay (how much will they pay to solve it?)

3. Geography:

- Domestic vs. international - Specific regions or countries - Whether regulatory environment is favorable

4. Business model:

- Transactional (one-time sales) - Recurring (subscription, membership) - Marketplace (you take a cut) - Freemium (free tier + paid tier)

The Positioning Framework: Extreme Focus

Here's the model most successful companies use:

Year 1-2: Nail a specific segment (70-80% focus)

Pick one customer type, one use case, one geography.

Go deep. Understand their problems better than anyone. Build product that's optimized for them, not a generic version.

Become the default choice in that segment.

Year 2-3: Expand to adjacent segments (15-20% focus)

Once you've dominated your core segment, you have:

  • Proven business model
  • Product that's becoming more mature
  • Revenue to fund expansion

Now expand to adjacent segments: similar customers, nearby use cases, new geographies.

Your existing product likely works well for adjacent segments because you've built it well for your core.

Year 3+: Leverage scale into new markets (5-10% focus)

With proven business model and brand, you can expand faster into new markets.

But you don't make it 50% of focus. You have adjacent segments that are still growing. Focus on what's working.

The Positioning Trap: Trying to Expand Too Fast

Most companies fail because they try to expand before they've dominated their core.

They see a new market opportunity. It's adjacent. Revenue looks big. They start building a product or entering the market.

Meanwhile, a focused competitor owns the core segment you invented. By the time you turn back, they've built moats, established relationships, and you can't catch them.

The principle: Don't be everything to everyone.

Be the best at one specific thing. Then expand from strength.

Positioning Examples

Salesforce (1999):
  • Customer: Mid-market enterprises (too small for Oracle, too big for nothing)
  • Use case: Sales team CRM
  • Geography: North America
  • Model: SaaS (monthly subscription, instead of enterprise license)

Very specific positioning.

Eventually expanded to: Large enterprises, adjacent business functions (marketing, customer service), international markets.

But they won their market by owning the sales CRM position with mid-market companies.

Slack (2012):
  • Customer: Tech companies with distributed teams
  • Use case: Team communication (replacing email)
  • Geography: Everywhere (internet-native)
  • Model: Freemium + paid teams

Very specific positioning.

Not "we're a communication tool for everyone."

"We're how tech teams communicate together."

This specific positioning allowed them to build deep features that mattered to their audience, build strong switching costs, and become a default.

Airbnb (2008):
  • Customer: Budget travelers
  • Use case: Affordable accommodation in expensive cities
  • Geography: San Francisco (then New York, then major cities)
  • Model: Marketplace (commission on bookings)

Very specific positioning.

Not "we're a hotel alternative for everyone."

"We're how budget travelers find affordable places to stay in expensive cities from people in those cities."

This specific positioning allowed them to build brand with travelers and hosts, overcome regulatory opposition by being customer-focused, and build switching costs.

Chapter 4: Execution Planning - From Strategy to Reality

Strategy is useless without execution.

Most strategies fail not because the strategy is wrong, but because execution fails.

Execution fails for these reasons:

1. Strategy isn't clear. Leadership can't articulate it in one sentence. Teams don't know if they're executing it.

2. Strategy conflicts with incentives. Leadership says "become more customer-centric" but measures profit (short-term) instead of customer satisfaction (long-term).

3. Strategy requires changes people don't want to make. Strategy might require cutting a business line, eliminating roles, or changing how people work. People resist.

4. Resources aren't allocated. Strategy requires money and people. But you don't actually reallocate budget to support it.

5. Strategy isn't integrated into operations. Strategy lives in a PowerPoint deck. Quarterly operations meetings don't reference it. OKRs don't align to it.

6. Leadership changes. New CEO comes in with new strategy. Or current leadership gets distracted by crisis.

Building an Executable Strategy: The Framework

Step 1: Articulate Strategy in One Sentence

Your strategy should be explainable in one sentence. If it takes a paragraph, it's not clear enough.

Examples:

  • "We're the most affordable ride-hailing option for price-sensitive urban commuters" (Uber's early strategy)
  • "We make premium products that command price premium through design and brand" (Apple)
  • "We're the fastest, cheapest option for e-commerce through operational efficiency" (Amazon)
  • "We become indispensable to teams by being the primary communication interface" (Slack)
Step 2: Identify the 2-3 Critical Initiatives That Make Strategy Real

Strategy stays abstract until you translate it into concrete initiatives.

If your strategy is "become customer-centric," then critical initiatives might be:

  • Implement NPS tracking and make NPS tied to executive compensation
  • Create a customer advisory board and meet quarterly
  • Reduce product release cycle from quarterly to monthly (get customer feedback faster)

If your strategy is "become the cost leader," critical initiatives might be:

  • Automate manufacturing processes (reduce labor costs)
  • Consolidate supplier relationships (volume discounts)
  • Streamline product line to highest-volume SKUs (reduce complexity)

Critical initiatives are not everything you do. They're the 2-3 things that, if you succeed, make the strategy real.

Step 3: Measure Progress Against Strategy

Establish metrics that indicate whether you're executing strategy.

Not just revenue and profit (everyone measures those). Measure strategy-specific metrics.

If your strategy is customer-centric:

  • NPS score (and trend)
  • Customer retention rate
  • Time to resolve customer issues

If your strategy is market dominance in a segment:

  • Market share in that segment
  • Win rate against specific competitors
  • Customer concentration (are you winning from diverse customers or few big ones?)
Step 4: Cascade Strategy to OKRs (Objectives and Key Results)

OKRs are how you operationalize strategy.

Each department's OKRs should ladder up to support critical initiatives.

Example:

Corporate Strategy: Become the customer-centric market leader in enterprise SaaS Critical Initiative: Increase NPS from 35 to 50 in next 18 months Engineering OKR:
  • Objective: Reduce time-to-value for customers
  • Key Results:
- Launch onboarding improvements reducing time-to-first-success from 30 days to 14 days

- Achieve 95% system uptime (currently 93%)

Sales OKR:
  • Objective: Improve customer selection (sell to customers who will be successful with us)
  • Key Results:
- Implement qualification criteria; disqualify low-fit deals

- Close rate on qualified deals increases from 25% to 35%

Customer Success OKR:
  • Objective: Increase customer success and retention
  • Key Results:
- Implement quarterly business reviews with top 50 customers

- Increase net retention rate from 90% to 95%

Each OKR ladder back to the critical initiative. And each department's focus is aligned with strategy.

Step 5: Reallocate Resources to Support Strategy

If you're serious about strategy, you need to fund it.

If your old strategy required:

  • 60% of budget to sales
  • 20% to product
  • 20% to operations

And your new strategy is to build a more valuable product in a narrower segment, you might reallocate to:

  • 40% to sales (more efficient with focus)
  • 35% to product (build deeper)
  • 25% to operations (streamline through focus)

This is painful. The sales leader loses resources. But it's necessary if you're serious about the new strategy.

Most strategies fail because leadership says "new strategy" but doesn't reallocate resources. So teams are still optimized for the old strategy.

Step 6: Align Compensation to Strategy

People optimize for what they're paid to optimize for.

If your strategy is customer-centric, but sales compensation is based on revenue, your salespeople will chase revenue (even low-quality revenue) that creates customer dissatisfaction.

Misaligned compensation is a poison pill for strategy execution.

Align:

  • CEO compensation: Long-term value creation (stock, not just cash)
  • Sales: Mix of revenue AND customer satisfaction (NPS, retention)
  • Product: Mix of feature delivery AND customer impact (usage, NPS)
  • Operations: Cost control AND quality (not just cost)

The Execution Cadence

Execution requires rhythm and discipline.

Weekly: Team stand-ups. Are we on track on the critical initiatives? Blockers? Next week's priorities? Monthly: Leadership reviews. Progress on critical initiatives and strategic OKRs. Reforecast. Course-correct if needed. Quarterly: Strategy review. Are we still confident in the strategy? Have market conditions changed? Do we need to adjust? Annually: Strategy reset. Year-end review of strategy execution. What worked? What didn't? What's next year's focus?

Chapter 5: Risk Mitigation - Preparing for What You Can't Predict

Even with perfect strategy, things go wrong.

Competitors move faster than expected. Market conditions change. Customers prioritize differently. Key people leave.

The best strategists don't assume their strategy will unfold perfectly. They identify risks and prepare for downside.

The Risk Framework: Three Categories

1. Market Risks

Demand doesn't materialize.

  • You invested in entering a market segment. Turns out adoption is slower, pricing is lower, or customers don't prioritize your solution.

Competitive response.

  • You launch a new product. Incumbent competitor responds by bundling competing feature into their core product, crushing your advantage.

Macro downturn.

  • Economy enters recession. Customers cut budgets. Your growth slows.

Regulatory change.

  • New regulation makes your business model unviable. (Happened to Uber in some jurisdictions.)
2. Execution Risks

Key people leave.

  • Your VP of Engineering takes a job at a competitor. Takes institutional knowledge with them.

Product doesn't work.

  • You bet on a new technology. It doesn't work as expected. You spent a year and $5M on it.

Customer concentration.

  • 50% of revenue comes from three customers. One leaves. Revenue collapses.
3. Financial Risks

Working capital crunch.

  • Growth requires working capital faster than you can fund it.

Refinancing risk.

  • Debt matures and you can't refinance.

Dilution.

  • You raise capital, but on bad terms. Dilutes your ownership significantly.

Risk Mitigation Strategies

For each major risk, identify:

1. Likelihood: How probable is this?

2. Impact: If this happens, how much damage? 3. Mitigation: What can we do to prevent this or reduce impact? Market Risk: Demand Doesn't Materialize

Mitigation:

  • Build and test with early customers before full commitment
  • Phased rollout instead of big bang (reduce bet size)
  • Flexible commitment from customers (don't lock in for years if demand is uncertain)
  • Rapid feedback loops (monthly customer meetings, not annual)

Example: If you're entering a new market segment, acquire 5-10 pilot customers with term contracts instead of full commitment. If pilots succeed, expand. If they don't, you've limited downside.

Market Risk: Competitive Response

Mitigation:

  • Build moats faster than competitors can replicate
  • Deepen customer relationships (switching costs)
  • Move so fast you gain significant advantage before they can respond
  • Choose positions where your strengths matter and their strengths don't

Example: You launch a new feature. Competitor is bigger and will likely copy it. So you don't bet on feature advantage alone. You bet on integration and customer relationships that take time to build, giving you a window to build advantage.

Execution Risk: Key Person Dependency

Mitigation:

  • Document processes and knowledge (not in people's heads)
  • Cross-train on critical functions
  • Build bench strength (have deputies for key roles)
  • Tie key people to company through equity and long-term vesting

Example: Your VP of Product is extraordinary but carries enormous institutional knowledge. Mitigation: Hire a strong deputy. Have them co-lead product initiatives. Create documentation of product philosophy and process. If VP of Product leaves, you have continuity.

Financial Risk: Rapid Working Capital Needs

Mitigation:

  • Model working capital requirements in growth forecast (don't just model revenue and profit)
  • Maintain cash buffer (3-6 months of operating expenses)
  • Negotiate favorable payment terms with customers and suppliers
  • Consider debt facilities as backup liquidity

Example: You forecast 50% growth. That requires $2M in working capital that you don't have. So you either: (a) reduce growth rate to match your ability to fund it, (b) raise capital, or (c) renegotiate terms (collect faster, pay slower).

Strategic Flexibility: The Hidden Advantage

The best defense against risk is flexibility.

Companies that have:

  • Diversified customer base (not dependent on few large customers)
  • Multiple revenue streams (not one bet)
  • Strong balance sheet (cash to absorb shocks)
  • Flexible cost structure (can reduce costs quickly if needed)

...survive downturns that destroy competitors with concentrated risk.

This argues for:

  • Building some customer diversity even as you focus on core segment (80/20, not 100/0)
  • Maintaining gross margins that fund R&D and provide cushion
  • Keeping some cash on the balance sheet (inefficient from capital perspective, but insurance)
  • Building flexibility into your cost structure (some fixed, but enough variable costs that you can adjust)

Chapter 6: Real Case Studies

Case Study 1: The Incumbent's Blind Spot

Company: Blockbuster Video

In 2000, Blockbuster was the dominant player in video rental. They had:

  • Brand (everyone knew Blockbuster)
  • Scale (5,000+ stores)
  • Distribution network (accessible everywhere)
  • Switching costs (loyalty program, convenience of nearby store)

They were unassailable. Or so it seemed.

The Disruption:

Netflix launched in 1997 with a different model:

  • Mail-delivery subscription
  • No late fees (customers hated late fees)
  • Convenient (no trip to store)

Blockbuster's response:

  • Ignored Netflix as irrelevant (mail? who wants mail delivery?)
  • Focused on protecting their core (stores, late fees as profit center)
  • Saw their threat but didn't believe it (mail-based model was slower)
What Actually Happened:

As internet improved, Netflix shifted to streaming. Suddenly they weren't just more convenient, they were enabling a fundamentally different experience (any movie, anytime, on any device).

Blockbuster's stores became a liability (expensive real estate, limited inventory, geographic constraints).

Netflix's streaming model became an asset (no inventory costs, global distribution, infinite "shelf space").

By the time Blockbuster realized the threat, Netflix had built scale, data, and brand that Blockbuster couldn't catch.

Blockbuster filed for bankruptcy in 2010.

The Strategic Lesson:

Blockbuster had a defensible strategy in their market (video rental stores). But they didn't understand that their market was being disrupted.

They optimized for defending their existing moat (stores, late fees) instead of asking: "What if the market for video rental disappears?"

For you: Don't assume your competitive advantage is immune to disruption. Regularly ask:
  • Is my market being disrupted? (Streaming disrupting physical media)
  • Are my moats becoming liabilities? (Stores = assets in physical world, liabilities in digital world)
  • Am I defending the right position, or defending against the wrong threat?

Case Study 2: The Disruptor's Bet

Company: Amazon (entering retail from e-commerce)

In 2010, Amazon was the dominant e-commerce player. But their share of retail was tiny (~0.5%).

Physical retail was dominated by Walmart, Target, Best Buy, etc.

The Bet:

Amazon decided to compete in physical retail.

Not by opening stores (that's the incumbent playbook). But by:

  • Building a data-driven logistics network (better supply chain than Walmart)
  • Obsessing on customer convenience (free shipping, fast delivery)
  • Combining with online (order online, pick up in store; return in store, etc.)
The Opposition:

Retail incumbents said: "Amazon doesn't understand retail. We're hardware. We're people. We're relationships. Amazon is online-only."

They saw Amazon as a threat to overcome, not a competitor in their core.

What Actually Happened:

Amazon didn't need to own stores (too capital-intensive, bad model). They needed to own:

  • Data on what customers want
  • Logistics to deliver it cheaply and fast
  • Experience that made buying from Amazon easier than from others

They built the capability to compete in retail without building the stores.

By 2020, Amazon's share of US retail grew to 15%+. Physical retailers were struggling.

The Strategic Lesson:

Amazon didn't beat Walmart by being Walmart-like. They beat Walmart by playing a different game:

  • Customer convenience (prime delivery) vs. store convenience
  • Data-driven discovery vs. shelf space discovery
  • Efficient logistics vs. geographic store network

They chose a position where their strengths (technology, data, logistics) mattered and physical retailers' strengths (real estate, store operations) didn't.

For you: When entering a market, don't try to beat incumbents at their game. Find a position where you can win. Choose battles where:
  • Your strengths matter
  • Incumbent's strengths don't matter
  • You can build moats faster than they can respond

Case Study 3: The Strategic Pivot

Company: Slack (from side project to category creator)

Slack wasn't Slack originally. It was an internal tool built by a company called Tiny Speck (gaming company).

When Tiny Speck's main product failed, they faced a choice:

  • Keep investing in gaming (competitive market, no clear advantage)
  • Shut down and go home
  • Spin out the internal tool that people loved (Slack) and see if it's a real product
The Bet:

CEO Stewart Butterfield decided to bet on Slack.

The Strategic Positioning:

Slack could have tried to be a broad communication platform (competing with Skype, Teams, etc.).

Instead, they positioned as:

  • "The messaging platform for teams"
  • Not replacing email globally, but becoming the team communication hub
  • For tech-first companies (early adopters)
  • With a focus on integration (connect all your tools to Slack)
What Made It Work:

1. Narrow positioning: Not "communication for everyone," but "team communication for tech companies."

2. Network effects: Slack's value increased as more team members joined and more integrations were added.

3. Switching costs: As teams integrated more tools into Slack and teams became dependent on it, switching became hard.

4. Execution: Ship features quickly, respond to user feedback, build integrations teams wanted.

5. Timing: Teams were becoming remote or distributed. Slack solved a real pain point at the right time.

What Makes This a Pivot:

Slack's early strategy (2013-2014) was different from Tiny Speck's strategy.

New team, new market, new positioning. Same underlying product, but completely different go-to-market.

The Strategic Lesson:

Be willing to pivot if evidence suggests a better path.

Butterfield could have said: "We're doubling down on gaming." Instead, he listened to evidence (internal tool is loved, main product failed) and pivoted.

Often the best strategy emerges from what's working, not from perfect planning.

For you: Strategy isn't destiny. Be willing to pivot based on evidence. What signals are you ignoring?

Appendix A: Industry Analysis Tools

Porter's Five Forces (Recap)

While this module goes beyond Porter, understanding his framework is still useful.

Five forces that determine attractiveness of a market:

1. Threat of new entrants: How hard is it for new competitors to enter?

2. Threat of substitutes: Can customers solve the problem differently? 3. Bargaining power of buyers: How much power do customers have? Can they negotiate price down? 4. Bargaining power of suppliers: How much power do suppliers have? 5. Rivalry among competitors: How intense is competitive rivalry?

High threat on all five = unattractive market (hard to be profitable).

Low threat on most = attractive market (easier to be profitable). Your strategic application:

Use Five Forces to understand industry structure, then use the frameworks in this module to determine how to build advantage within that structure.

Strategic Positioning Matrix Revisited

Plot each competitor on three dimensions:

Differentiation vs. Cost Leadership vs. Focus

Cost leadership: "We're the cheapest."

Differentiation: "We're the best." Focus: "We're the best for [specific segment]."

Most viable strategies are:

  • Cost leadership in a large market (Walmart, Southwest, IKEA)
  • Differentiation in a specific segment (Tesla for electric vehicles, Apple for premium electronics)
  • Differentiation AND focus (luxury brands are usually focused on high-income segment)

Avoid:

  • Being stuck in the middle (not the cheapest, not the best)
  • Trying to be low-cost and premium simultaneously (hard to execute)
