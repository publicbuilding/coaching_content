## I. What Are Porter's Five Forces?
**Length:** 0.4 pages | **Time:** 1 minute

### The Framework
Michael Porter's Five Forces is a tool to analyze competitive intensity in any industry.

**The Five Forces:**
1. **Threat of new competitors** (Can new players enter?)
2. **Bargaining power of suppliers** (Can suppliers squeeze us?)
3. **Bargaining power of customers** (Can customers squeeze us?)
4. **Threat of substitutes** (Can customers find alternatives?)
5. **Competitive rivalry** (How intense is competition?)

### Why It Matters
- Shows how profitable/attractive your industry is
- Reveals where you're most vulnerable
- Guides strategic decisions (what to defend, where to invest)
- Predicts industry evolution

### The Reality Check
If you understand these forces, you can navigate them. If you don't, you'll be blindsided.

## II. Force #1: Threat of New Competitors
**Length:** 0.6 pages | **Time:** 1.5 minutes

### The Question
How easy is it for new players to enter and compete in your industry?

### What Makes Entry Hard (Good for You)
- **High capital requirements:** Need $10M+ to start (e.g., auto manufacturing)
- **Proprietary technology/IP:** Patents, trade secrets competitors can't copy
- **Established brand/relationships:** Customers stick with incumbents
- **Regulatory barriers:** Government requires licenses, certifications
- **Economies of scale:** Can't be cost-competitive at small scale
- **Switching costs:** Expensive/difficult for customers to switch suppliers

### What Makes Entry Easy (Dangerous for You)
- **Low capital requirements:** Can start with $10K (e.g., consulting, freelancing)
- **No IP moat:** Anyone can copy your model
- **Customers don't care about brand:** Willing to try newcomers
- **No regulations:** No licensing required
- **No scale advantage:** A solo operator can compete
- **Low switching costs:** Customers can easily switch

### Examples
**Hard to enter:**
- Commercial aircraft manufacturing (billions in R&D, regulatory approval)
- Pharmaceutical (patents, FDA approval, 10+ years)
- Commercial banking (regulatory capital requirements)

**Easy to enter:**
- Social media (anyone can start an account and build audience)
- E-commerce (no inventory needed with dropshipping)
- Personal services (any person can start freelancing)

### Strategic Implications
- **High barriers:** Fewer competitors, more stable margins
- **Low barriers:** Constant new entrants, pressure to improve
- **Response:** Create barriers (build brand, IP, scale, switching costs)

### Assessment Questions
- [ ] How much capital does a competitor need to enter?
- [ ] Can they copy your technology/model?
- [ ] Do customers care about brand/relationships (switching costs)?
- [ ] Are there regulatory requirements protecting you?
- [ ] Do you have scale advantages?

**Score: 1 (low threat) to 5 (high threat)**

## III. Force #2: Bargaining Power of Suppliers
**Length:** 0.6 pages | **Time:** 1.5 minutes

### The Question
How much power do your suppliers have to raise prices or reduce supply?

### What Makes Suppliers Powerful (Bad for You)
- **Few suppliers:** Limited options, they control prices
- **Differentiated products:** Their product is unique, hard to replace
- **High switching costs:** Expensive/difficult to switch to another supplier
- **Credible threat to forward integrate:** Supplier could become your competitor
- **They don't depend on you:** You're not important to them

### What Makes Suppliers Weak (Good for You)
- **Many suppliers:** Lots of options, competition keeps prices down
- **Commodity products:** Their product is interchangeable
- **Low switching costs:** Easy to switch suppliers
- **Can't forward integrate:** No threat they'll compete with you
- **They depend on you:** You're important to their business

### Examples
**Powerful suppliers:**
- Intel (CPUs): Only two real chipmakers; highly specialized; tech companies need their products
- Agricultural processors: Limited number of quality suppliers; farmers have few alternatives
- Oil refiners: Limited refining capacity; very capital intensive to build

**Weak suppliers:**
- Janitorial services: Thousands of suppliers; commodity service; easy to switch
- Packaging suppliers: Standardized products; multiple competitive options
- Fabric/textiles: Global commodity markets; multiple suppliers worldwide

### Strategic Implications
- **Powerful suppliers:** Your costs are at risk; margins compressed
- **Weak suppliers:** Can negotiate favorable terms; stable, predictable costs
- **Response:** Diversify suppliers, build internal capability, backward integrate, long-term partnerships

### Assessment Questions
- [ ] How many suppliers exist for each critical input?
- [ ] How differentiated are their products?
- [ ] How much would it cost to switch?
- [ ] Could they become your competitor?
- [ ] How important is your business to them?

**Score: 1 (weak threat) to 5 (strong threat)**

## IV. Force #3: Bargaining Power of Customers
**Length:** 0.6 pages | **Time:** 1.5 minutes

### The Question
How much power do your customers have to demand lower prices or better terms?

### What Makes Customers Powerful (Bad for You)
- **Many alternative suppliers:** Customers have options
- **Low switching costs:** Easy for them to switch to competitor
- **Standardized products:** Your product is interchangeable
- **They're large/important:** You depend on them (concentration risk)
- **They can backward integrate:** Threaten to do it themselves

### What Makes Customers Weak (Good for You)
- **Few alternative suppliers:** Limited options for them
- **High switching costs:** Expensive/difficult to switch
- **Unique/critical product:** You're the only one who solves their problem
- **Many small customers:** You don't depend on any one customer
- **Can't do it themselves:** Complex, capital intensive, they have no option

### Examples
**Powerful customers:**
- Retailers buying from suppliers: Walmart, Amazon have massive power; suppliers fight for shelf space; commoditized products
- Automotive parts suppliers: Make one specific part; manufacturers have multiple supplier options
- Grain farmers selling to consolidators: Thousands of farmers; few large buyers

**Weak customers:**
- Cloud infrastructure customers buying from AWS: Few real alternatives; switching is complex; AWS has unique scale/capability
- SaaS customers in niche markets: Few providers; switching is disruptive; mission-critical
- Enterprise software customers: Years of implementation; deep integration; high switching cost

### Strategic Implications
- **Powerful customers:** Price pressure; margins compressed; must differentiate
- **Weak customers:** Can command premium pricing; stable margins; defensible business
- **Response:** Differentiate, build switching costs, create ecosystem lock-in, niches where you're the only option

### Assessment Questions
- [ ] How many competitors do customers have to choose from?
- [ ] How much would it cost them to switch?
- [ ] Is your product unique or standardized?
- [ ] Are you dependent on large customers?
- [ ] Could they do it themselves?

**Score: 1 (weak threat) to 5 (strong threat)**

## V. Force #4: Threat of Substitutes
**Length:** 0.6 pages | **Time:** 1.5 minutes

### The Question
Can customers solve the problem in a completely different way?

### What Makes Substitutes Threatening (Bad for You)
- **Cheaper alternative exists:** Different product/service solves same problem for less
- **Performance parity:** Alternative works just as well
- **Customers switching:** Trend toward substitutes accelerating
- **High switching costs don't apply:** Substitutes overcome your advantages
- **Substitute technology improving:** Gap closing over time

### What Makes Substitutes Non-Threatening (Good for You)
- **No real alternatives:** Customers must use your solution
- **Substitutes are inferior:** Don't solve the problem as well
- **Substitutes are more expensive:** Your solution is the best value
- **Substitutes require customer behavior change:** Too disruptive
- **You compete on performance:** Better than substitutes in what matters

### Examples
**Major substitute threats:**
- **Video calls replaced face-to-face meetings:** Zoom, Teams vs. travel
- **Email replaced memos/phone calls:** Different solution to communication problem
- **LED lighting replaced incandescent:** Same end outcome (light), different technology, cheaper
- **Electric cars replace gas cars:** Different solution to transportation

**Minor substitute threats:**
- **Gas grills vs. charcoal:** Some substitution, but for different customer needs
- **Premium coffee vs. instant:** Different solution, but lower price point not appealing to premium segment
- **Streaming vs. cable:** Substitute for video content, but not fully (some content exclusive)

### Strategic Implications
- **Major substitutes:** Business model at risk; must adapt or differentiate
- **Minor substitutes:** Manageable; focus on segments where you're superior
- **Response:** Improve performance, lower cost, make switching to substitute difficult, monitor trends, innovate preemptively

### Assessment Questions
- [ ] What's an alternative way customers could solve this problem?
- [ ] How many substitutes exist?
- [ ] Are substitutes cheaper, better, or both?
- [ ] How many customers are switching to substitutes?
- [ ] Is the trend accelerating or stabilizing?

**Score: 1 (low threat) to 5 (high threat)**

## VI. Force #5: Competitive Rivalry
**Length:** 0.5 pages | **Time:** 1 minute

### The Question
How intense is direct competition among existing competitors?

### What Makes Rivalry Intense (Bad for You)
- **Many competitors:** Fragmented market
- **Similar products:** Hard to differentiate
- **Slow industry growth:** Zero-sum game; one's gain is another's loss
- **High fixed costs:** Must sell volume to break even
- **Low switching costs:** Customers shop around
- **Competitors pursuing different strategies:** Unpredictable, chaotic

### What Makes Rivalry Less Intense (Good for You)
- **Few competitors:** Concentrated market
- **Differentiated products:** Clear competitive advantage
- **Fast industry growth:** Growing pie; less fighting
- **Low fixed costs:** Can be profitable at lower volumes
- **High switching costs:** Customers stick
- **Competitors similar:** Predictable, stable competition

### Examples
**Intense rivalry:**
- Airline industry: Many competitors, low differentiation, fight on price, high fixed costs, customers shop for lowest fare
- Retail/grocery: Many competitors, commoditized products, slow growth, extreme price competition

**Low rivalry:**
- Aircraft manufacturing: Two main competitors (Boeing, Airbus), differentiated, high barriers, government relationships, stable pricing
- Market leader in niche software: Few competitors, high switching costs, strong differentiation, stable market

### Strategic Implications
- **Intense rivalry:** Price pressure, hard to profit, need differentiation or cost advantage
- **Low rivalry:** Can command premium pricing, stable margins, defensible position
- **Response:** Differentiate, build cost advantage, consolidate market, find niches, build customer loyalty

### Assessment Questions
- [ ] How many direct competitors exist?
- [ ] How differentiated are the products?
- [ ] Is industry growing or mature?
- [ ] Who has cost advantage?
- [ ] Is anyone threatening to disrupt?

**Score: 1 (low rivalry) to 5 (intense rivalry)**

## VII. Putting It Together: Your Industry Analysis
**Length:** 0.5 pages | **Time:** 1 minute

### The Assessment Matrix
```
Force                          Threat Level    Strategic Implication
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
1. New Competitors             ☐ Low  ☐ High   ______________________
2. Supplier Power              ☐ Low  ☐ High   ______________________
3. Customer Power              ☐ Low  ☐ High   ______________________
4. Substitutes                 ☐ Low  ☐ High   ______________________
5. Competitive Rivalry         ☐ Low  ☐ High   ______________________

Overall Industry Attractiveness: ☐ Attractive ☐ Average ☐ Unattractive
```

### Interpreting Your Results
- **All forces low:** Attractive industry, defensible position, invest
- **Mixed forces:** Pockets of opportunity; focus on areas with advantages
- **Most/all forces high:** Unattractive industry; must differentiate or exit

### Strategic Response Framework
For each high-threat force, ask:
1. "Can we eliminate this threat?" (Change strategy, innovate, differentiate)
2. "Can we reduce this threat?" (Build advantage, partnerships, scale)
3. "Must we accept this threat?" (Accept lower margins, niche positioning)
