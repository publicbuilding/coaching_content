## **The Leadership Crisis at Scale**

[VISUAL: Graph showing: Founder's energy and effectiveness at 5 people (high), stays high, then at 50 people (suddenly drops), company chaos appears]

"Early on, you're the culture. Your energy is the company's energy. Your values drive decisions. Your vision is what everyone works toward.

It works because there are only five people. Everyone knows you. Everyone can ask you anything. You're in every meeting. You make decisions fast.

Then you grow to 20 people. It still mostly works. You're stretched thin but it works.

Then you grow to 40 people.

Now something changes. You physically can't be in every meeting. You can't know what everyone is working on. You can't be the culture anymore because there are people in the company who rarely see you.

Those people create their own micro-cultures. Engineering has one vibe. Sales has another. Support is separate.

Instead of one unified culture, you have silos.

And here's what kills most founders: They try to be the culture anyway.

They work 80-hour weeks. They're in every meeting. They're making every decision. They're burnt out.

And it doesn't work. Because at 50 people, one person can't be the culture anymore. You need systems."

[VISUAL: Show the stress: Founder exhausted, burned out, not sleeping, surrounded by problems, overwhelmed]

"I worked with a founder who was at 45 people. She was working constantly. She told me: 'I'm dying. If I'm not in every decision, things fall apart.'

I asked her: 'What happens if you take a week off?'

She said: 'The company would fall apart.'

That's not leadership. That's a single point of failure.

Real leadership is: If you take a week off, the company still runs. Better even. Because people step up."

## **Section 1: Delegation as a Leadership Skill**

[VISUAL: Founder making all decisions (overworked), then delegating (team empowered, founder relaxed)]

"Delegation terrifies most founders because they built the company. They think nobody can do it as well as them.

They're usually right. Nobody will do it exactly as them.

But somebody will do it well enough. And that somebody will do it while the founder does something else the founder is the only one who can do.

Here's what happens without delegation:

You stay bottleneck. Every decision waits for you. Every problem comes to you. You work 80 hours and the company runs at 40% efficiency because everyone is waiting on you.

With delegation:

Your team makes decisions. Some are wrong. You learn from it. Next time they get it right. Your team works 40 hours and the company runs at 100% efficiency because decisions are being made at all levels.

Which is faster?

The second one.

Here's how to delegate:

Step one: Pick the right person.

Who on your team is good at the type of decision you're delegating? Who cares about the outcome? Who's capable?

Don't delegate to the person you need to keep busy. Delegate to the person who can actually do it.

Step two: Be clear about the scope.

'You're deciding on all engineering hiring. Budget is $X. Timeline is this quarter. You're not deciding on ops hiring—that's Jane. You report back monthly on who you've hired and why.'

Clarity is critical. Vague delegation creates problems.

Step three: Let them do it.

This is the hard part. They'll do it differently than you would. That's okay. That's the whole point.

If you keep overruling their decisions, they'll stop making decisions. You've trained them to wait for you.

Step four: Provide feedback, not direction.

After the decision is made, review it with them: 'Here's what I thought worked. Here's what I might have done differently. Next time, consider this.'

You're coaching them to get better. Not taking over."

[VISUAL: Delegation framework showing: Person → Scope → Authority to decide → Feedback, not overruling]

"The outcome: Your team gets better at decision-making. You have more time for strategy. And you're not the single point of failure."

## **Section 2: Culture Systems—How to Make Culture Scalable**

[VISUAL: Culture document, values, rituals, processes, all documented and reinforced]

"Here's what most founders get wrong about culture: They think it's about the founder being a good person.

It's not. It's about systems.

Real culture is: The way we hire, the way we make decisions, the way we communicate, the way we celebrate wins, the way we handle failure.

Culture isn't vague. It's systematic.

Let me show you what that looks like.

First: Values-driven hiring.

You've identified your core values. Let's say: We move fast, we care about customers, we're honest.

Now, when you're hiring, you interview for those values. Not just skills.

You ask: 'Tell me about a time you moved fast to solve a customer problem.'

You listen for whether they actually did that. You ask references: 'Is this person someone we can trust to be honest even when it's hard?'

You hire people who embody the values. Over time, everyone in the company embodies the values. Culture compounds.

What kills culture: Hiring brilliant jerks who don't fit your values. One person who doesn't respect the culture poisons the well.

So the first system is: Only hire people who fit your values, even if they're less skilled.

Second: Values-driven decisions.

When you make decisions, you reference your values.

'We have an opportunity to take a big customer who's asking us to compromise on X. Our value is integrity. We're saying no.'

Everyone sees the decision. They understand: We actually mean our values.

When leaders make decisions against their stated values, culture breaks. People don't trust leadership.

So the second system is: Make decisions publicly that demonstrate your values.

Third: Rituals that reinforce culture.

Weekly all-hands where everyone shares wins. This reinforces: We celebrate each other's wins.

Monthly customer stories shared by the team. This reinforces: We care about customers.

Annual offsite where you talk about mission and values. This reinforces: We're aligned on why we're here.

Rituals create rhythm. Rhythm creates culture.

So the third system is: Design rituals that reinforce what matters.

Fourth: Values-driven feedback.

When someone does great work that matches your values, you acknowledge it publicly: 'Sarah shipped that feature the way she always does—with incredible speed and care for the customer. That's exactly what we need more of.'

When someone violates your values, you address it: 'This decision was made without customer input. That's against our values. Here's how we fix it next time.'

Over time, people internalize the values. They don't need you to tell them what to do. They reference the values themselves.

So the fourth system is: Give feedback that reinforces values.

Fifth: Onboarding that teaches culture.

When someone new joins, they need to understand your culture immediately.

First day: Talk about mission and values. Why do we exist? What do we stand for?

First week: Have them shadow someone who embodies the culture. They see it in action.

First month: New person leads a meeting or ships something that showcases the culture. They're part of it now.

Most founders skip this. New person shows up, gets assigned a task, figures it out. Six months later they don't understand the culture.

So the fifth system is: Teach culture in onboarding.

When you have these systems in place, culture doesn't depend on you being the culture anymore. Culture is built into how the company operates."

[VISUAL: Show culture systems in action: hiring, decisions, rituals, feedback, onboarding]

"And here's the beautiful part: These systems actually make your job easier. Because culture self-reinforces."

## **Section 3: Staying Connected When You Can't Be Everywhere**

[VISUAL: Founder at office, connecting with people, listening, present, not controlling]

"As you scale, you can't know everyone deeply. But you can stay connected.

This is critical. Leaders who lose connection with their team lose the ability to lead. They become distant executives. And nobody wants to work for a distant executive.

Here's how to stay connected when you're at 50+ people:

First: Monthly one-on-ones with your direct reports.

Even if you have five to eight direct reports, spend 30 minutes with each one every month.

What are they working on? How are they feeling? What do they need from you?

You don't solve their problems. You listen. You unblock them. You show them they matter.

This is not optional. This is how you know what's actually happening in the company.

Second: Quarterly town halls where you listen more than you talk.

You share company updates. Then you open it up: 'What questions do you have? What are you thinking about?'

You listen. You learn what people actually care about. You stay grounded in reality instead of living in your own narrative.

Third: Monthly office hours.

Anyone can book 15 minutes on your calendar to talk about anything.

Most founders are scared to do this. 'I don't have time.'

You have to make time. This is how you know if people trust you. This is how you stay connected.

Fourth: Walk around.

Before meetings, after meetings, spend 10 minutes just talking to people. Ask them what they're working on. Show genuine interest.

Most leaders hide in their offices. The best leaders are visible.

Fifth: Tell your story and listen to theirs.

Share why you started this company. What were you trying to solve? Share a mistake you made and what you learned.

Then ask people: 'Why did you join? What drew you here?'

When people know your story and you know theirs, connection exists.

None of this takes extra time. It just requires showing up."

[VISUAL: Founder having genuine conversations with team members, listening intently]

"The founders who maintain culture at scale do this. The ones who lose culture are the ones who become too important to be present.

Don't be that founder."

## **Section 4: Handling the Hard Conversations**

[VISUAL: Founder in a difficult conversation—someone isn't fitting the culture, needs to be addressed]

"As you scale, you'll have to have difficult conversations.

Someone is great at their job but doesn't fit the culture. They're brilliant but they undermine the values.

These conversations are hard. Most founders avoid them.

But avoiding them poisons the culture worse than anything else.

Here's how to have them:

First: Get clear on what's happening.

'Our value is collaborative. You frequently make decisions without input from others. That's not aligned. I want to understand what's going on.'

Be specific. Not vague.

Second: Listen to their side.

Maybe they think collaboration is slow. Maybe they have pressures you don't understand.

Listen for what's actually happening underneath the behavior.

Third: Give them a choice.

'We need to see you acting more collaboratively. Here's what that means specifically. Are you willing to work on this?'

Most people will say yes. Some will say no.

Fourth: Follow up.

If they said yes, in two weeks you check in: 'I've noticed you involving others more. That's good. Here's what I still want to see change.'

You're coaching them. You're giving them a real chance.

Fifth: If it doesn't change, make a hard move.

If they won't change and they won't leave, you let them go.

This is painful. But not doing it is worse. Because everyone else sees: We say we have values but we don't enforce them. So nobody believes in the values.

And when you do let someone go who doesn't fit, you tell the company why:

'We let X go because their approach to collaboration didn't align with our values. We care more about our values than we care about any one person's skills.'

Everyone sees: We actually mean this.

Culture gets stronger."

[VISUAL: Show clear communication: What needs to change, why, timeline, followup, then if needed, transition]

"The hard conversations are the leadership work. The easy conversations are fine. The hard ones are where culture is made or broken."

## **Section 5: Your Evolution as a Leader**

[VISUAL: Timeline showing: Founder as doer at 5 people, as manager at 20 people, as leader at 50+ people]

"Here's what most founders don't understand: Leadership at scale is a completely different skill than leadership at startup.

At five people, you need to be great at the work. Great at execution.

At 20 people, you need to be great at delegation. Great at clarity. You're a manager.

At 50+ people, you need to be great at vision. Great at culture. Great at tough calls. You're a leader.

If you don't evolve, the company breaks.

The founder who was great at execution often sucks at vision. They get stuck in the work. They can't think strategically because they're still trying to do the work.

The founder who was great at delegation often sucks at culture building. They can task out work but they don't think about the systems that hold the company together.

The best founders evolve. They get coaching. They read. They study other leaders. They deliberately learn the next skill set.

You need to ask yourself: What's the skill I need to develop right now?

If you're at 10 people: Delegation.
If you're at 30 people: Culture systems.
If you're at 50+ people: Strategic vision and tough decision-making.

Work on it intentionally."

[VISUAL: Show before/after—founder struggling with old approach, then succeeding with evolved approach]

"And here's the thing: Getting better at the next level sometimes means getting worse at the current level.

You delegate something you were doing well. It's messy for a bit. That's okay. You're investing in scale.

Most founders can't tolerate that. So they don't delegate. They stay good at the old level and bad at the new level. The company breaks."
