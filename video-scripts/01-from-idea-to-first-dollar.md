## **The Problem: Why Most Founders Get It Wrong**

[VISUAL: Screen shows statistics: "67% of startups fail due to lack of market need" - Show pie chart]

"Here's what most founders do: They fall in love with their solution instead of being obsessed with their customer's problem. I see this every single week. Someone comes to me with a 'revolutionary app' that they've spent three months building. I ask them: 'How many paying customers do you have?'

The answer: Zero.

But here's what kills me—they never even asked anyone if they wanted it. They assumed. They built. They launched. And they crashed.

The hard truth? Your opinion about what the market needs is worth zero. Maybe negative. Your customer's opinion is worth everything."

## **Section 1: Validation—Before You Code One Line**

[VISUAL: Show a simple interview setup—someone talking to a potential customer in a coffee shop]

"Validation doesn't mean a survey. Surveys lie. People tell you what they think you want to hear.

Real validation is this: Get in front of your ideal customer and talk to them. Not about your idea. About their problem.

Here's the script—it's simple:

'I'm working on something in your space. Can I buy you coffee and ask you some questions?'

That's it. When they say yes, don't pitch. Listen.

Ask questions like:
- 'What's the worst part of your day around this problem?'
- 'How are you solving it today?'
- 'How much would you pay to make this go away?'

Listen for the pain. Listen for frequency. Listen for whether they've already tried to solve it or if they've just given up.

You're not validating your idea. You're discovering if a real problem exists that real people will pay to solve."

[VISUAL: Notebook showing customer interview notes, checkmarks next to common pain points]

"I recommend: Talk to 30 customers before you write a single line of code.

Why 30? By customer 15, you'll see patterns. By customer 25, you won't hear anything new. By 30, you'll know if this is real or just three people who care.

This takes two weeks if you're focused. Two weeks.

Compare that to six months of building the wrong thing."

## **Section 2: The MVP—Minimum Viable Product Is Not a Feature Set**

[VISUAL: Laptop screen showing simple landing page with just headline and email signup]

"Here's what founders get wrong about MVP: They think minimum viable product means 'the product with fewer features.'

Wrong.

Minimum viable product is the smallest thing you can build to prove one hypothesis: Does anyone care?

I've seen founders spend months on an MVP that was still too much. They added things because they seemed 'important.' They added features because 'people might want it.' They added complexity because they couldn't resist.

Stop. The MVP is not beautiful. It's not complete. It's barely usable. And that's the point."

[VISUAL: Timeline showing: Weeks 1-2: Landing page + email signup. Then arrow to Weeks 3-4: Hand-delivery of solution. Then arrow to Weeks 5-6: First revenue]

"Your first MVP might be a Google Form. Seriously. 'Enter your problem. I'll solve it manually for you.' You manually deliver the solution in Google Sheets or email. If someone is willing to pay for manual delivery, then and only then do you automate.

I worked with a B2B founder who spent eight months building 'the platform.' When we validated, I suggested: 'Can you just do this in Zapier for now?' He did. He got his first customer in week two.

That customer gave him the real spec for what the platform should be. He built it faster and it solved real problems instead of imagined ones.

Your first customers aren't paying for perfection. They're paying for solving their immediate crisis. They'll tell you what perfection looks like as you go."

## **Section 3: Finding Your First 100 Users**

[VISUAL: Map of the internet with different channels highlighted: Reddit, Twitter, LinkedIn, communities, cold email]

"This is where everyone gets mystical. 'How do I find customers?' As if there's some secret hack.

There's no hack. There's just work.

Your first hundred users come from three places:

Number one: Places where your customer already hangs out.

If you're selling to developers, you're going to Reddit, Hacker News, Twitter dev communities. Not because you're 'going viral'—because your customer is literally there, solving their problem with existing solutions right now.

If you're selling to founders, you're in founder communities: Twitter, Y Combinator discussions, founder Slack groups. 

If you're selling to fitness coaches, you're in fitness coach Facebook groups and coaching forums.

Your customer is already grouped somewhere online. Go there. Be helpful first. Offer value first. Mention your thing second."

[VISUAL: Screenshot of a Reddit thread with a helpful comment that includes a link to a solution]

"Number two: Cold outreach to look-alike customers.

This is emails, DMs, inbound from your personal network. I know, it feels gross. It's not.

Here's why: You're reaching out to someone solving the exact problem you solve. You're offering to save them time and money. That's not spam. That's helpful.

But—and this is critical—you have to be specific. Generic cold outreach has a response rate of less than one percent. Personalized outreach has a response rate of five to fifteen percent.

Subject line matters. The first sentence matters. And you need to have actually used their product or known something specific about them.

Instead of: 'Hi Sarah, are you open to trying a new email tool?'

Try: 'Hi Sarah, I saw your launch of [specific product]. The way you're handling [specific challenge] is interesting—we've solved a similar problem for companies like [industry type]. Worth a fifteen-minute conversation?'

That's the difference between spam and discovery."

[VISUAL: Spreadsheet showing cold email campaign: Names, personalized notes, responses, meetings booked]

"Number three: Partnerships and referrals.

Who else already serves your customer? If you're selling to SaaS founders, tax CPAs already serve that customer. Design agencies already serve them. Finance coaches already serve them.

How can you help them help their customers? Can they refer? Can they bundle? Can they recommend?

Your first hundred users? One third might be partners. One third might be direct outreach. One third might be communities you participate in."

## **Section 4: Closing Your First Deals**

[VISUAL: Two people on a video call, one talking, the other taking notes]

"Okay, you've found someone interested. Now comes the part that terrifies most founders: asking for money.

Let's be real: Your first customers will not buy from a slick sales process. They'll buy because you've shown them you understand their problem better than anyone else and you can solve it.

That's it.

Here's how you close:

Step one: Get on a real conversation—phone or video.

An email can't close a deal. A slide deck can't close a deal. Only a conversation can.

Step two: Before you pitch anything, confirm the problem.

'So when we last talked, you mentioned X was your biggest pain. Is that still accurate? What's changed?'

They'll often tell you new information that changes everything. Listen to it."

[VISUAL: Notebook showing: Problem statement, impact (how much this costs them), desired outcome, timeline]

"Step three: Explain your solution in terms of their outcome, not your features.

Not: 'Our tool has API integrations and real-time webhooks.'

Instead: 'You'll never manually upload customer data again. It syncs automatically. Every morning your team starts with accurate data.'

The difference? One is features. One is freedom from their pain.

Step four: Give them a path to yes.

'Here's what we can do: You use it for three weeks. If your team isn't saving five hours a week like they should, you don't pay. If they are? You're $2,000 for the month.'

Specific. Backed by a guarantee. No friction.

Step five: Handle objections by asking questions.

They say: 'It's too expensive.'

You say: 'If I could reduce that cost and still deliver the same result, would that work?'

Or: 'What would the cost have to be?'

They're rarely saying no forever. They're saying no to your current offer. Find out what yes looks like."

[VISUAL: Text overlay showing first payment confirmation, money arriving in account]

"Your first deal might be rough. You might give them a discount. You might solve something custom. That's fine. The goal isn't perfect deal terms. The goal is proving someone will pay.

Once you have one customer paying, everything changes. You're not a founder with an idea anymore. You're a founder with a business."

## **The Sequence (In Practice)**

[VISUAL: Timeline graphic showing: Week 1-2: Validation, Week 3-4: MVP Build, Week 5-8: Finding First 100, Week 9-12: First Sales]

"Here's what a real timeline looks like:

Weeks 1-2: Validate the problem with 30 conversations.

Weeks 3-4: Build the smallest thing you can hand-deliver.

Weeks 5-8: Find your first 100 potential customers and have initial conversations.

Weeks 9-12: Close your first deals.

Three months from idea to revenue.

Is it fast? Yes. Is it the fastest it could be? No. But most founders take nine months to eighteen months to get here. They're taking the scenic route."
