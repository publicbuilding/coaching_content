## **Why Founders Avoid Sales (And Why That's Killing You)**

[VISUAL: Poll results or survey showing: "90% of founders avoid sales conversations"]

"Most founders hate sales. Let me tell you why.

First, it feels pushy. You think sales means tricking people into buying something they don't want. So you avoid it. You keep your hands clean. You just make the product better.

But that's not sales. That's a fantasy version of sales that doesn't exist. Real sales is: 'Here's a problem you have. Here's how we solve it. Here's the cost. Do you want it?'

That's not pushy. That's clear. That's helpful.

The second reason founders avoid sales: Rejection stings.

When an engineer ships a feature and it gets ignored, that's disappointing. When a founder pitches a customer and they say no, that feels personal. Because it is. You're putting yourself out there.

But here's what I've learned: The best founders are the ones who hear 'no' fifty times and keep going. Because fifty nos means fifty pieces of feedback. And maybe the fifty-first is a yes.

The third reason: Founders often conflate sales with the sales process.

Your founder can't close a million deals a year. But your sales team can. You think: 'If I have to do sales, I can't scale.' So you don't do sales. And your company doesn't scale anyway.

Wrong. You do sales until you've figured out what works. Then you hire people to replicate what works. That's the machine."

[VISUAL: Show the difference: Founder avoiding sales (no growth) vs Founder doing sales then handing off (growth accelerates)]

"Let me be crystal clear: If you're not willing to sell, you can't scale.

Not because you personally have to do all the selling forever. But because you have to be good enough at sales to know what works. You have to be able to teach your VP of Sales what that is. You have to be able to recognize when it's not working.

That requires you to sell."

## **Section 1: Understanding Product-Market Fit and Why It Matters**

[VISUAL: Graph showing product-market fit: Before (flat growth), after (hockey stick)]

"Before you build a sales machine, you need to know you have something worth selling.

Product-market fit is probably the most overused term in startups. But here's what it actually means: Your customer wants to use your product more than they want their money.

Not 'it's nice to have.' Not 'it solves a problem.' Not 'it's the best in category.' 

They want it. They'll fight for it. They'll pay for it. They'll tell their friends about it.

How do you know you have it? 

Your customer acquisition cost is low because people recommend you. Your churn is low because people don't want to leave. Your expansion revenue is growing because customers buy more from you over time.

Here's the danger: Most founders think they have product-market fit when they don't.

You've got 100 customers? Great. But 60 came from your personal network. That's founder-led sales, not product-market fit. The product isn't pulling customers in. You're pushing them.

You get those customers because they know you, believe in you, want to help you. Not because the product is irresistible.

This is crucial because it changes how you sell.

If you don't have product-market fit, you can still sell. But you have to sell harder. Your sales process has to be more consultative. You have to customize more. Your close rate will be lower.

If you do have product-market fit, your sales process is shorter. Customers are already pre-sold. Your sales person is just qualifying and closing.

The mistake most founders make: They try to scale before they have product-market fit. They hire a sales team who's supposed to do all the selling. But there's nothing worth selling.

The sales team fails. The founder blames sales. 'Maybe we need a better sales person.'

Wrong. You need a better product."

[VISUAL: Two path diagrams: Path A (weak product-market fit, hire sales team, sales team fails) vs Path B (strong product-market fit, hire sales team, growth accelerates)]

"Here's what I recommend: Before you hire your first salesperson, you need to personally close at least fifty deals.

Not fifty trials. Fifty paid customers. Fifty deals where you asked for money and got it.

Why fifty? It's enough to see patterns. To understand objections. To know what works. After fifty, you can teach someone else."

## **Section 2: Building Your Sales Process**

[VISUAL: Funnel diagram showing: Leads → Qualified → Discovery → Proposal → Close]

"Most founders don't have a sales process. They have a sales wish.

'I hope this person buys.'

That's not a process.

A sales process is: Here's how leads come in. Here's how we qualify them. Here's how we understand their problem. Here's how we propose. Here's how we close. Here's how we stay in touch if they're not ready yet.

Your first process will be simple. Too simple, probably. That's fine. You'll improve it as you go.

Here's a real process I saw work:

Step one: Inbound lead comes from web or referral.

Email goes out: 'Thanks for reaching out. I'd love to understand your situation better. Do you have 15 minutes this week for a call?'

That's it. Not a sales pitch. Just a meeting request.

Step two: Qualification call.

You have 15 minutes. In that call, you're answering one question: Is this person a real prospect?

They have the problem you solve. They have budget. They have timeline. They're not just kicking tires.

If yes, move to step three. If no, politely pass: 'Doesn't sound like it's the right time. Let me know if that changes.'

Don't waste time on bad fits.

Step three: Discovery call.

30 minutes. Go deep on their problem.

'Walk me through your current process.' 'What's the impact if it stays broken?' 'What would the perfect solution look like?' 'How would you measure success?'

You're listening 70% of this call. Speaking 30%.

Most founders talk too much. Let them talk. Their answer reveals everything.

Step four: Proposal.

You send a proposal that addresses their specific problem, not a generic pitch.

'Based on our conversation, here's what I'd recommend: Implementation, timeline, cost, expected outcomes.'

Don't send a proposal until you know they want it.

Step five: Close.

You get on a call: 'I sent over a proposal. What are your thoughts?'

Most people will buy or object. For objections, you handle them: 'That makes sense. What would need to change for this to work?'

If they're still uncertain: 'I want to make sure we're only moving forward if this is the right move. Are you ready to start, or do you need to think about it?'

Giving them permission to say no actually makes them more likely to say yes. Weird but true.

The key: Your process assumes they might say no. That's healthy. It's not desperate. It's not pushy.

It's just a clear path from lead to customer."

[VISUAL: Screenshot of actual sales emails showing the process in action]

"Now, the metrics you track:

Leads per week: How many inbound?
Qualification rate: What % of leads are real?
Discovery rate: What % of qualified leads say yes to a discovery call?
Proposal rate: What % of discoveries get a proposal?
Close rate: What % of proposals close?

When you multiply these together, you get your deal velocity.

Let's say:
- 50 leads per week
- 40% qualify (20 qualified)
- 50% say yes to discovery (10 discoveries)
- 70% get a proposal (7 proposals)
- 50% close (3.5 deals)

So you're closing 3.5 deals a week if your ACV is $5,000, that's $17,500 MRR.

When one of these metrics drops, you know where to fix it.

If your close rate dropped from 50% to 30%, the problem isn't 'we need more leads.' The problem is 'our discovery process is finding unqualified leads.'

Most founders don't track this. So they're blind. They just know 'sales is bad' without knowing why."

## **Section 3: Hiring Your First Sales Person**

[VISUAL: Job posting for VP of Sales, then interview process, then new salesperson starting]

"Once you've personally closed fifty deals and you have a repeatable process, you can hire someone.

The biggest mistake: Hiring a sales leader when you need a sales executor.

You think: 'I need a VP of Sales who can build the team.'

But you don't have a repeatable process yet. You need someone who can execute the process you've figured out. Then, after they've proven they can execute, you promote them to lead or you hire someone else to lead.

Most founders hire a VP of Sales who then tries to change your whole approach. It becomes their thing, not the company's thing. And it usually doesn't work.

Here's who to hire:

You want someone who's closed at least 100 deals in your industry or a similar industry.

You want someone who's worked for a founder or at an early-stage company. They'll move fast. They won't need bureaucracy.

You want someone who believes in your product. Not just as a job, as something they'd actually use.

You want someone who's coachable and humble. They're joining a company with no sales team. There's going to be constant adjustment.

In interviews, ask: 'Tell me about your best close. What did you do? What was tricky? How did you overcome it?'

Listen for detail. Listen for whether they problem-solved or just did their script. Listen for whether they actually cared about the customer's outcome.

The best salespeople I know aren't pushy. They're consultative. They care. They want to solve the problem.

Weird for a salesperson? Yeah. But that's what works."

[VISUAL: First sales hire on day one, and then months later with a team building momentum]

"One more thing: Have them shadow you for your first month.

They don't pitch. They watch. They see how you do discovery. How you handle objections. How you close.

Then they take over conversations. You're there but they're driving. You give feedback after.

After a month of this, they're ready to fly solo.

Most founders skip this. They hire someone, throw them at the market, and wonder why they fail. Your first salesperson should replicate your process, not reinvent it."

## **Section 4: Scaling the Machine**

[VISUAL: Sales team growing, processes documented, dashboards showing growth metrics]

"Once your first salesperson is hitting quota, you hire number two.

But you don't give them to the first salesperson to manage yet. You still manage them. They both follow the same process. You compare results.

When both are consistent, then you hire a sales leader. Someone who can manage a team of three to five.

The process of scaling sales is:

One person nails it. Two people nail it. Three to five people nail it. Then someone leads those people without doing sales themselves.

Most founders skip steps. They hire someone to lead a team that doesn't exist yet. That person either has to learn your process and execute it and then manage others (impossible) or they try to bring in their own process (doesn't work because you haven't nailed yours yet).

Slow is fast here.

Here's the other thing: Document everything.

When salesperson number two joins, salesperson number one shouldn't have to explain the process verbally. It should be written down.

'Here's the discovery template.' 'Here's the objection handling.' 'Here's how we price.' 'Here's why we qualified this way.'

This scales the machine beyond your first salesperson.

Over time, you can optimize the process. But you first need to understand what's working. That takes real data from multiple people executing the same way."

[VISUAL: Sales dashboard showing: Pipeline, conversion rates, revenue per rep, trends over time]

"The end goal is a machine where:

- Leads come in (from inbound, referral, cold outreach—we automate this)
- Sales team processes them through a known funnel
- Your conversion rate and deal size are predictable
- You can forecast revenue 90 days out with accuracy

When you get here, you're not managing individual sales interactions anymore. You're managing a process. You're optimizing metrics.

That's when you can step back and build the next thing."
