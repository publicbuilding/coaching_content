# The True Cost of a Bad Hire

## The Myth

Managers often think about bad hires in terms of salary. "We paid him $60k and got nothing. That's expensive."

That math is wrong. And it's dangerously wrong—because it leads you to underestimate how much bad hiring actually costs you.

The real cost isn't what you paid them. It's everything else that unraveled because they were there.

---

## What a Bad Hire Actually Costs

Let's walk through a real example.

You hire a sales rep. Salary + benefits = $80k/year. He's been with you 8 months and it's clear: he's not hitting targets, he's not coachable, and he's actually *hurting* your culture.

**The obvious cost:** $80k × (8 months / 12) = $53,333

**But that's just the beginning.**

---

### 1. Productivity Loss of Your Team

He joined at the 15-person company. To onboard him properly, your best people spent time:

- Training him on your systems
- Working alongside him on calls
- Giving him feedback (multiple times)
- Covering his pipeline when he fell behind

That's your best people not focusing on *their* work.

Conservative estimate: 3 senior people spent 10% of their time on him over 8 months.

- 3 people × 40 hours/week × 40 weeks (accounting for that 8 months) × 10% = 480 hours
- Loaded cost per hour (salary + benefits + overhead): ~$75/hour
- **Productivity loss: $36,000**

---

### 2. Opportunity Cost (Revenue You Didn't Make)

Your sales rep was supposed to close deals. He didn't. How much revenue was lost?

If a typical rep closes $500k in annual deals, 8 months of nonperformance = $333k in potential revenue that never materialized.

What's your margin on that revenue? If it's 30%, you lost $100k in profit.

**Opportunity cost: $100,000**

---

### 3. Customer Damage

He was on customer calls. He made promises he couldn't keep. He was unprepared. Customers felt the sloppiness.

One customer you'd had for 3 years decided to switch vendors because of his poor handoff. That was a $60k/year account.

**Customer impact: $60,000 in lost recurring revenue**

---

### 4. Hiring and Firing Costs

Now you have to hire again.

- Recruiter fee (20% of salary): $16,000
- Your time in interviewing (50 hours × $75/hour): $3,750
- Onboarding your replacement: ~40 hours of team time = $3,000
- Severance/legal (to part ways cleanly): $2,000

**Direct hiring costs: $24,750**

---

### 5. The Morale Tax

Your team watched this play out. They watched him underperform. They watched management try to coach him. They watched it take months to make a decision.

Three people started looking at other jobs. One quit (you'll replace him, more costs). The other two's productivity is down 5% because they're demoralized and interviewing elsewhere.

This is hard to quantify, but it's real.

**Conservative morale impact: $15,000** (in lost productivity from the three people over the next 6 months)

---

### 6. Reputation and Hiring

When you fire someone, they talk. In competitive industries, reputation matters. "That company doesn't know what they're doing" or "They hired me and then gave up on me" gets around.

Makes future recruiting slightly harder. Talent you could've hired goes elsewhere.

**Long-term recruiting friction: $5,000-$15,000**

---

## The Total Bill

Let's add it up:

| Cost Category | Amount |
|---|---|
| Salary + benefits paid | $53,333 |
| Team productivity loss | $36,000 |
| Lost opportunity revenue | $100,000 |
| Lost customer account | $60,000 |
| Replacement hiring costs | $24,750 |
| Morale and retention impact | $15,000 |
| Recruiting friction | $10,000 |
| **TOTAL** | **$299,083** |

You paid $53,333 to hire someone who cost you nearly $300k.

That's what a bad hire actually costs.

---

## Why This Matters

This isn't just about individual bad hires. If you're a scaling company, every bad hire compounds.

Let's say you're at 30 people and you hire 5 new people per year (to scale). If just 20% of them are bad hires (1 out of 5), you're bleeding $300k+ per year in drag.

**Now multiply that across 3-5 years of scaling. That's $1-2M in value destruction.**

That's not an accident. That's a strategic failure in how you hire.

---

## The Hard Truth: Early Warnings

Most bad hires show red flags within the first 30 days. Not 8 months. 30 days.

They miss deadlines. They avoid feedback. They blame circumstances. They're unprepared. They don't ask clarifying questions. They overpromise.

**The companies that save money aren't the ones that "give people more time." They're the ones that act fast.**

If someone isn't working out at day 30, you have a choice:

1. **Invest heavily in fixing it** (more coaching, clearer role, structured feedback). This only works if the person is coachable. Most bad hires aren't.

2. **Make a change quickly.** Yes, it feels harsh. But the longer you wait, the more it costs.

---

## How to Avoid This

### 1. Hire for Coachability, Not Just Skills

A smart person who won't listen is worse than a less experienced person who's hungry to improve.

### 2. Set Clear 30-Day Expectations

What does "good performance" look like? Make it specific. If they don't hit it, you have your answer.

### 3. Check References Thoroughly

Not a 5-minute call. Real conversations with people who've managed them. Ask: "What does she struggle with?" "Is he coachable?" "Why did he leave?"

### 4. Run Real Probation Periods

Some companies do 90 days, but many treat it as theater. If performance isn't there at 60 days, start the exit conversation.

### 5. Involve the Team in Hiring

Your team knows what good looks like. They'll spot culture misalignment or skill gaps faster than any interviewer.

### 6. Define What "Good" Looks Like

Too many hiring processes have vague criteria. Get specific: "closes 2 deals per month," "responds to customers within 4 hours," "mentors one junior person."
