# The 3 Questions That Close Every Deal

## The Problem With Sales Pitches

A sales rep finishes her pitch. She's covered every feature. She's answered questions. She's walked through the ROI.

She says: "So, what do you think?"

The prospect says: "Let me think about it and get back to you."

She never hears back.

This happens because the rep *presented* rather than *diagnosed*. She told the prospect why her solution was great. But she never confirmed that the prospect actually *needed* it.

---

## The Insight

A deal doesn't close because the sales person was smart or persuasive.

A deal closes because the prospect reached a conclusion: **"We have a problem. This solution solves it. We should buy it."**

Your job isn't to convince them. Your job is to help them *reach that conclusion.*

And that happens by asking the right questions.

---

## The Three Questions

### Question 1: "What Would Change If This Problem Went Away?"

Before you present a solution, confirm that the prospect actually sees the problem as worth fixing.

This question flips the conversation from "your solution" to "their outcome."

**The prospect answers:** "If we solved this, our team would spend 20 hours less per week on manual tasks. We could take on more clients without hiring. We'd probably do another $200k in revenue."

Now you know:

- The problem matters (20 hours/week is real)
- There's a financial benefit ($200k revenue)
- They've thought about the implications (can do more with same people)

**Wrong answer:** "Honestly, nothing would change. We'd just be more efficient."

That's a signal: *This isn't a priority for them.* Don't sell. Ask more diagnostic questions or walk away.

**How to ask it:**

Not: "Do you want to solve this?"

Better: "Let's assume we solved this. What would actually be different about your business? Walk me through a day after we fix it."

This forces them to imagine the outcome. And in the imagining, they convince *themselves* it's important.

---

### Question 2: "How Are You Currently Solving This?"

This reveals what they're already paying (money, time, frustration) to solve the problem.

**Example:**

Prospect: "We're using three different tools, switching between them, and manually entering data into a spreadsheet."

Sales: "How much time per week?"

Prospect: "Maybe 15 hours. And we still make mistakes. Costs us about $2,000 a month in corrections and rework."

Now you know:

- They're already spending money on a solution (even if it's broken)
- They have a real cost ($2,000/month)
- The problem is causing damage (mistakes)

If your solution costs $1,500/month and eliminates that $2,000 cost *plus* frees up 15 hours, the math is obvious to them.

**How to ask it:**

Not: "What tools are you using?"

Better: "Walk me through how you're currently solving this. What's working? What's frustrating?"

This opens them up to complain. Let them. Every complaint is a pain point.

---

### Question 3: "What Would Prevent You From Fixing This?"

This is the unspoken objection question.

Instead of waiting for them to say "no," you ask them to voice the concerns.

**Example:**

Sales: "If this solution saved you $2,000/month and freed up 15 hours, what would prevent you from moving forward?"

Prospect might say:

- "We'd need IT to approve it" (process objection)
- "Our team has used the other tool for 3 years" (change management objection)
- "We'd need to see it working first" (risk objection)
- "We don't have budget approved yet" (financial objection)

Now you know what the *real* objection is.

And instead of you trying to overcome an objection they haven't voiced, you can address it directly:

"Budget—got it. Here's what I'd suggest: let's do a 30-day pilot with three people. Costs $500. If it delivers what we've outlined—15 hours saved, zero manual entry errors—then we know it's worth the investment for the full team. Sound fair?"

**How to ask it:**

Not: "Do you have any objections?"

Better: "In an ideal world, we'd have this live in 30 days. What would get in the way of that?"

By asking what would prevent them, you're not putting them on the defensive. You're asking them to be realistic about logistics.

## The Flow

Here's how these three questions flow in a sales conversation:

**You:** "What would change if this problem went away?"

**Prospect:** "We'd save time, be more accurate, probably grow revenue."

**You:** "Great. How are you currently solving this?"

**Prospect:** "We use three tools and manual entry. It's a nightmare."

**You:** "How much time and money is that costing you?"

**Prospect:** "$2,000/month in errors, 15 hours per week in manual work."

**You:** "Okay. So if we solved this—saved the time, saved the money, eliminated the errors—what would prevent you from moving forward?"

**Prospect:** "Honestly, we'd need to get budget approved and make sure the team can learn the new system."

**You:** "Budget is [month/time]. And on the learning curve—our onboarding is 2 hours of training plus ongoing support. We have customers who were up to speed in a week. Does that address your concern?"

**Prospect:** "That actually sounds doable."

**You:** "Great. Here's what I'm thinking: Let's start with a 30-day pilot with your most time-intensive team. Costs X. We measure the results against what we've outlined. If it delivers, we expand. Does that feel like a reasonable first step?"

**Prospect:** "Yeah, I'd be willing to try that."

---

## Why This Works

1. **You're not selling.** You're helping them think through their problem.

2. **They reach the conclusion.** "We have a problem, it costs us money, your solution fixes it."

3. **You've addressed objections before they become objections.** Instead of them saying "no," you've already said "here's how we overcome that."

4. **They feel heard.** You asked about their situation, their constraints, their concerns. They feel understood.

5. **The close becomes natural.** By the time you suggest moving forward, it feels obvious to them.

## The Common Mistakes

### Skipping to Question 3

Sales rep: "We're the best solution. What would prevent you from buying?"

This feels aggressive. The prospect doesn't want to commit yet.

**Fix:** Ask Q1 and Q2 first. Build the case. *Then* ask Q3.

### Not Listening to the Answer

Prospect answers Question 1: "Honestly, this isn't a huge priority for us right now."

Sales rep: "Oh, but here's why it should be..." (Tries to convince them instead of listening.)

**Fix:** If Question 1 reveals it's not a priority, don't push. Say "Got it. Let's keep this on your radar. When timing shifts, let me know." And move on.

### Making Q2 About Competitor Bashing

Prospect: "We're using [competitor's tool]."

Sales: "Yeah, their tool is terrible. Everyone complains about it."

Now the prospect is defensive about their choice.

**Fix:** "How's that working for you? What do you like about it? What frustrates you?" Stay curious, not judgmental.

### Pitching Before Asking

You meet them. Before you've asked anything, you're explaining your solution.

They're not engaged because you haven't confirmed they have the problem.

**Fix:** Ask questions first. Get them talking about their situation. *Then* show how you solve it.

## Adapting for Different Sale Types

### Complex B2B Sales (Long Cycles, Multiple Decision-Makers)

Use these three questions with each stakeholder:

- The operations person: "What would change if this process was automated?"
- The CFO: "What's the cost of the current process?"
- The CEO: "What would prevent us from moving forward?"

Each person has different answers, different priorities. Ask all three questions to all stakeholders.

### Transactional Sales (E-Commerce, Quick Decisions)

Shorten it:

- "What brought you to our site?" (What problem are they solving?)
- "Are you replacing another tool or starting from scratch?" (Current state)
- "What would need to be true for you to buy today?" (Objection/timeline)

You can do this in a 3-minute conversation.

### SaaS Free Trial Conversion

- "What were you hoping to accomplish?" (Q1)
- "How were you solving this before the trial?" (Q2)
- "What would need to happen for you to keep using this?" (Q3, modified)

Do this in week 2 of their trial, when they've experienced enough to have an opinion.

## The Psychology

These three questions work because they align with how humans actually make decisions.

1. **First:** We confirm there's a problem worth solving.
2. **Second:** We acknowledge the current cost (what we're already paying).
3. **Third:** We identify concerns, then address them.

It's not manipulation. It's honest diagnosis. And it's *way* more effective than pitching.

---

## Your Next Sales Call

Try this on your next opportunity:

1. Ask Question 1: "What would change?"
2. Listen. Ask follow-ups.
3. Ask Question 2: "How are you currently solving this?"
4. Listen. Quantify the cost if they don't.
5. Ask Question 3: "What would prevent you from fixing this?"
6. Address the objection they raise.
7. Propose a next step that matches their concern.

Don't pitch unless they ask.

Watch what happens.
