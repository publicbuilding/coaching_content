# Reading Financial Statements in 10 Minutes

## Why You Need This (Even If You Have a CFO)

You don't need to be an accountant to read a financial statement. But as a leader, you *do* need to understand what's actually happening in your business.

Consider this: A company's revenue looks great. Up 25% year-over-year. The CEO is celebrating. But the CFO knows something the CEO is missing: profit margins are collapsing. They're growing fast but losing money on every sale. Within 18 months, the company runs out of cash despite hitting their revenue target.

This catastrophe was visible in the numbers. It just required reading three documents correctly.

There are three core financial statements. Learn these, and you'll see what's actually happening in any business—your own or one you're evaluating.

---

## The Three Financial Statements

Think of them as three perspectives on the same business:

1. **Income Statement** (P&L): *"Are we making money?"*
2. **Balance Sheet**: *"What do we own and owe?"*
3. **Cash Flow Statement**: *"Do we actually have cash?"*

Earnings can lie. Cash doesn't.

---

## Statement #1: The Income Statement (P&L)

### What It Answers
*"Is the business profitable?"*

### The Basic Structure

```
Revenue                          $100,000
  - Cost of Goods Sold           ($40,000)
  _______________
Gross Profit                      $60,000  (60% margin)

  - Operating Expenses
    Salaries                      ($20,000)
    Marketing                     ($8,000)
    Facilities                    ($5,000)
  _______________
Operating Profit (EBIT)           $27,000

  - Interest Expense              ($2,000)
  - Taxes                         ($6,250)
  _______________
Net Income (The Bottom Line)      $18,750
```

### What Each Line Means

**Revenue**: Total money coming in. This is *not* profit; it's the top line.

**Cost of Goods Sold (COGS)**: The direct cost to create what you sell. For a product company: materials, labor, shipping. For a service firm: usually labor hours billed to clients. *This is NOT overhead.*

**Gross Profit (Revenue - COGS)**: What's left after you cover the cost of delivery. This shows whether your core business model works. A 60% gross margin means you keep 60 cents of every dollar before running the company.

**Operating Expenses**: The overhead to keep the lights on—salaries, rent, marketing, insurance, software licenses. These are necessary but don't directly produce a service or product.

**Operating Profit (EBIT)**: Your actual business performance, before financing costs and taxes. This is what you'd tell an investor about your business health.

**Interest Expense**: The cost of debt. Low debt = low interest. High debt = this line item hurts.

**Taxes**: The government's cut.

**Net Income**: What's left. The bottom line. This is profit.

### What to Look For

**Gross margin trending**: Is COGS growing faster than revenue? If yes, your unit economics are worsening—dangerous long-term.

**Operating expenses as % of revenue**: Healthy range depends on industry, but watch the trend. If OpEx is creeping up as a % of revenue, efficiency is declining.

**Profitability line**: A company can be revenue-positive but profit-negative (or even cash-flow positive but profit-negative—more on that later). Watch the bottom line.

### Real Example
A SaaS company reports $10M revenue. Sounds great. But:
- COGS (cloud hosting, support): $3M
- OpEx (sales, marketing, R&D): $8M
- Net loss: -$1M

The company is growing but unprofitable. The P&L shows the problem immediately.

## Statement #2: The Balance Sheet

### What It Answers
*"What is this company worth? What does it own and owe?"*

### The Basic Structure

```
ASSETS (What we own)
  Current Assets
    Cash                         $15,000
    Accounts Receivable          $20,000
    Inventory                    $10,000
  Total Current Assets           $45,000
  
  Non-Current Assets
    Equipment                    $30,000
    Intangible Assets             $5,000
  _______________
Total Assets                      $80,000

LIABILITIES (What we owe)
  Current Liabilities
    Accounts Payable             $10,000
    Short-term Debt              $5,000
  Total Current Liabilities      $15,000
  
  Long-term Liabilities
    Long-term Debt              $20,000
  _______________
Total Liabilities                $35,000

EQUITY (Owner's stake)
  Common Stock                   $10,000
  Retained Earnings              $35,000
  _______________
Total Equity                     $45,000

Total Liabilities + Equity       $80,000
```

**The Golden Rule**: Assets = Liabilities + Equity. Always.

### What Each Section Means

**Current Assets**: Cash and things you can turn into cash within a year. Accounts Receivable = money customers owe you.

**Non-Current Assets**: Long-term stuff. Equipment, buildings, patents.

**Current Liabilities**: Bills due within 12 months. Accounts Payable = money you owe suppliers.

**Long-term Debt**: Loans maturing beyond 12 months.

**Equity**: What's left if you sold everything and paid off all debt. This is "owner's stake."

### What to Look For

**Working Capital** (Current Assets - Current Liabilities): Can you pay your bills? A positive number means you can. Healthy businesses have positive working capital.

**Debt-to-Equity Ratio** (Total Debt / Total Equity): How leveraged is the company? High debt = riskier. Low debt = conservative (but possibly not optimized).

**Asset Turnover**: How efficiently does the company use its assets to generate revenue? Higher is better. Calculated as Revenue / Total Assets.

### Real Example
A manufacturing firm has $100M in assets but only $50M in annual revenue. Asset turnover is 0.5—they're not using assets efficiently. A leaner competitor with $50M assets generating $50M revenue (turnover of 1.0) is better managed.

### The Trick: Goodwill

Be cautious of "Goodwill" on the balance sheet. It represents the premium paid when acquiring another company. It's often large but intangible. It *can* impair (lose value), which suddenly reduces equity and crushes shareholder value. Watch for it.

## Statement #3: The Cash Flow Statement

### What It Answers
*"Is the company *actually* generating cash?"*

This is the most important statement for survival. A company can be profitable on paper but run out of cash.

### The Basic Structure

```
OPERATING ACTIVITIES
  Net Income                      $18,750
  Add back: Depreciation          $2,000
  Changes in Working Capital      ($5,000)
  _______________
Cash from Operations              $15,750

INVESTING ACTIVITIES
  Capital Expenditures            ($8,000)
  Asset Sales                     $2,000
  _______________
Cash from Investing               ($6,000)

FINANCING ACTIVITIES
  Debt Borrowed                   $10,000
  Debt Repaid                     ($5,000)
  Dividends Paid                  ($2,000)
  _______________
Cash from Financing               $3,000

NET CHANGE IN CASH
  Beginning Cash                  $10,000
  + Operations                    $15,750
  + Investing                     ($6,000)
  + Financing                     $3,000
  _______________
Ending Cash                       $22,750
```

### What It Means

**Operating Activities**: Cash generated by running the business. This should be positive. If net income is positive but cash from operations is negative, something is wrong (usually: growing receivables or inventory).

**Investing Activities**: Cash spent on long-term assets (equipment, acquisitions) or received from selling assets. Usually negative for growing companies.

**Financing Activities**: Cash from debt, equity, or returned to shareholders. Can be positive or negative.

### Why This Matters Most

You can earn $10M in profit and still go bankrupt if customers don't pay you. A fast-growing company might have massive receivables (money owed to them) that inflate profit on the P&L but drain actual cash.

**The deadly scenario:**
- P&L shows +$5M profit ✅
- Cash flow statement shows -$2M cash ❌
- Company runs out of cash despite being "profitable"

This happens constantly to fast-growing startups.

### Real Example
A consulting firm books $1M in revenue when they complete a project. The P&L immediately recognizes the profit. But the client pays 60 days later. For 60 days, the company has profit on paper but no cash. If they hire people and pay rent based on the P&L, they can run out of cash before the invoice is paid.

The Cash Flow Statement shows the truth: operating cash is still negative until the money arrives.

## How to Use These Together

**The P&L tells you if the business model works.** If gross margins are terrible and operating expenses are out of control, the business is broken, not just temporarily unprofitable.

**The Balance Sheet tells you if the company can survive.** High debt + low equity = risk. Poor working capital = liquidity crisis incoming.

**The Cash Flow Statement tells you if the company will survive.** Positive operating cash flow means the business funds itself. Negative operating cash flow means it's burning money and needs external funding.

### The Health Check

Ask these three questions:

1. **P&L**: Is the company profitable or on a clear path to profitability?
2. **Balance Sheet**: Does it have healthy working capital and manageable debt?
3. **Cash Flow**: Is operating cash flow positive?

If all three are strong: the company is healthy.

If one is weak: watch closely.

If two are weak: red flag.

If all three are weak: the company is in trouble.

## Common Traps to Avoid

### Trap #1: Confusing Revenue with Profit
Revenue can grow 50% while profit shrinks. Watch both.

### Trap #2: Ignoring Working Capital
A company with great profit and terrible working capital will run out of cash. It happens fast.

### Trap #3: Trusting Only the Bottom Line
Earnings can be manipulated through accounting choices. The Balance Sheet and Cash Flow Statement are harder to fake.

### Trap #4: Forgetting About Growth Investments
A young company investing heavily in R&D or sales will show low profit. That's *intentional*. Don't judge it by short-term P&L alone.

### Trap #5: Ignoring Debt Maturity
A company with $50M in debt is fine if it matures over 10 years. It's in crisis if it's due next year.

---

## Key Takeaways

- **P&L** shows if the business model works (profit)
- **Balance Sheet** shows if the company can survive (assets, liabilities, equity)
- **Cash Flow** shows if the company will survive (actual cash generated)
- All three are necessary. Don't trust just one.
- Investors and analysts obsess over these. You should too.

## A Quick Ratio Cheat Sheet

| **Ratio** | **Formula** | **What It Means** |
|---|---|---|
| **Gross Margin** | (Revenue - COGS) / Revenue | Unit economics health |
| **Operating Margin** | Operating Profit / Revenue | Core business efficiency |
| **Net Margin** | Net Income / Revenue | Bottom-line profitability |
| **Current Ratio** | Current Assets / Current Liabilities | Short-term liquidity (>1 is good) |
| **Debt-to-Equity** | Total Debt / Total Equity | Leverage level (lower = less risky) |
| **Return on Equity (ROE)** | Net Income / Total Equity | How well the company uses shareholder capital |
