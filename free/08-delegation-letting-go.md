# Delegation: Why Your Best Leaders Know When to Let Go

## The Core Problem

A CEO is doing everything. Closing deals. Fixing bugs. Making hiring decisions. Writing blog posts. Managing finances.

She works 60-hour weeks. Her team sits idle, waiting for her to make decisions.

She thinks: "If I don't do it, it won't get done right."

What she doesn't see: **Her team can't grow because she won't let them. The company can't scale because she's the bottleneck. And she's burning out.**

This is the delegation trap. And high-achievers fall into it hardest because they've built their success on doing excellent work themselves.

That skill—excellence in execution—becomes a liability when it prevents them from leading.

---

## The Three Types of Work

Not all work is created equal. And delegation isn't about dumping work on people. It's about understanding which work *you* need to do, and which work *someone else* should do.

### Type 1: Strategic Work (Only You Can Do This)

This is work that:
- Defines the direction of the company
- Requires your institutional knowledge
- Involves high-stakes decisions
- Sets culture and values
- Involves your key relationships (investors, board, major customers)

**Examples:**
- Fundraising strategy and investor conversations
- Company direction and product roadmap decisions
- Hiring executives
- Major partnership negotiations
- Setting compensation strategy

**Rule:** You do this. You can't delegate it. You can get input, but final decisions are yours.

---

### Type 2: Operational Work (You Need To Oversee, But Shouldn't Execute)

This is work that:
- Is important but not unique to you
- Has clear standards
- Can be done by someone competent
- Needs your oversight but not your hands

**Examples:**
- Closing typical sales deals (your sales team should do this)
- Day-to-day hiring decisions
- Budget management
- Weekly team updates
- Feature prioritization (you set the framework; PM executes)
- Customer support escalations

**Rule:** You set the standards, review results, and step in when quality drops. But you don't do this day-to-day.

---

### Type 3: Tactical Work (Delegate This)

This is work that:
- Has clear processes
- Doesn't require your judgment
- Doesn't involve strategic decisions
- Can be taught to someone competent

**Examples:**
- Scheduling meetings
- Taking notes in meetings
- Expense reports
- Email management
- Social media posting
- Booking travel
- Routine reporting

**Rule:** Delegate this. Completely. Your time is too valuable.

## Why Leaders Don't Delegate

### "I Can Do It Faster"

True. You probably can. On this task. Today.

But multiply that across 20 tasks per week × 50 weeks = 1,000 tasks you're doing that someone else could do.

That's 2,000+ hours per year of your time.

What could you do with 2,000 hours? Close bigger deals? Build strategy? Mentor your team?

**The math:** If you're earning $200/hour (rough calculation for a CEO), and a team member can do the task at $40/hour, it costs you $160 of opportunity cost *every time* you do it instead of them.

### "They Won't Do It As Well"

Probably not the first time. The fifth time? They'll be better than you.

Most people improve with practice. You started somewhere too.

The question isn't "Will they do it as well as I would?" It's "Will they do it well enough? And will they improve?"

If the answer is yes, delegate.

### "I Like Doing It"

This is the honest one. And it's dangerous.

You became a leader because you were good at your previous job. So you keep doing it.

But your job changed. You're no longer a doer. You're a leader. And a leader's job is to make your team better, not to be the best doer.

If you're spending 30% of your time doing work you love, you're neglecting the work that actually scales your company.

### "My Team Isn't Ready"

Maybe not. But they won't get ready if you don't give them the work.

Competence comes from doing. Not from waiting for perfect readiness.

## The Delegation Framework

Here's how to delegate without it becoming a nightmare:

### Step 1: Define the Standard

Before you delegate, define what "done right" looks like.

Not vague: "Handle customer support."

Specific: "Respond to support emails within 4 hours with a solution or a timeline to solution. Escalate to me if the customer is angry or asking for a refund. Document every interaction in the system. Weekly summary of issues on Friday."

When the standard is clear, the person knows what success looks like. And you know what to evaluate.

### Step 2: Delegate With Authority

Don't delegate the task. Delegate the responsibility and the authority to make decisions within that domain.

Wrong: "Here's a deal. Close it for me."

Right: "Your goal is to close deals under $50k. You have authority to make pricing decisions within 10% of list price and commit to delivery timelines that don't exceed 3 months. Anything above $50k or outside those parameters, you loop me in. How do you want to handle it?"

When they have authority, they're invested. They make better decisions. They're not constantly asking permission.

### Step 3: Set Clear Checkpoints

Don't disappear.

Set a schedule for review. Weekly? Biweekly? Depends on the task.

"Let's do a 15-minute check-in every Wednesday. You'll show me what you've accomplished, any blockers, and what's next. I'll give feedback and we'll make any adjustments."

Regular checkpoints mean:

- You catch problems early
- They know you care about quality
- It's not a surprise review; it's expected
- You can course-correct

### Step 4: Let Them Fail Small

In the early days, the stakes should be low.

Don't delegate a $500k deal. Delegate a $50k deal.

Don't delegate company hiring. Delegate recruiting.

Let them make small mistakes. Learn. Improve. *Then* escalate their responsibilities.

### Step 5: Adjust and Repeat

After a few checkpoints, adjust based on reality.

If they're crushing it, expand their authority. Give them bigger deals, harder problems.

If they're struggling, don't pull it back. Instead, ask: "What support do you need? What process would help you?"

Maybe they need clearer templates. Maybe they need to shadow you once. Maybe they need a bit more time to learn.

Support them through the learning curve. Don't just take the work back.

## What Happens When You Delegate Well

1. **Your team grows.** They're doing harder work, learning faster, becoming more capable.

2. **You get time back.** Now you can focus on strategy instead of execution.

3. **Decisions happen faster.** Your team makes decisions in their domain without waiting for you. Projects move faster.

4. **Your company scales.** If you're the bottleneck, the company caps at what one person can do. Remove the bottleneck, and growth accelerates.

5. **Your team feels trusted.** When you give someone authority and responsibility, they feel trusted. Trust builds loyalty and motivation.

6. **You discover who your leaders are.** As people take on more, you see who rises up. Those are your future leaders.

## The Common Mistakes

### Delegating Too Much, Too Fast

You decide to delegate everything. You dump a pile of work on someone with no support, no clear standards, no checkpoints.

They fail. You take it back. "See? I was right to do it myself."

Wrong lesson. You delegated poorly, not wrongly.

**Fix:** Delegate one thing at a time. Support it. Let them succeed.

### Rescuing Too Early

They're struggling. You jump in and take over.

Now they learned: "If I struggle, the boss does it for me. I don't need to figure it out."

**Fix:** Let them struggle a bit. Ask "What do you need from me?" before you take it back.

### Changing Standards Mid-Project

You delegate, then realize you'd do it differently. So you critique their approach even though they're doing it to the standard you set.

They get whiplash. They stop trying because the goalposts keep moving.

**Fix:** Agree on the standard upfront. Don't move it unless the standard itself was wrong.

### Not Celebrating Wins

They accomplish something. You note it and move on to the next thing.

They don't get that their growth matters to you.

**Fix:** Actually celebrate. "I noticed you closed that deal with a 10% discount instead of 15%, and negotiated a 6-month payment plan. Smart moves. That's exactly the decision-making I want to see."

## The Real Skill

Delegation isn't about dumping work. It's about:

- **Identifying** what work actually matters (Type 1 vs. Type 2 vs. Type 3)
- **Developing** people by giving them work that stretches them
- **Trusting** them with authority and responsibility
- **Supporting** them through the learning curve
- **Praising** the wins and learning from the losses

This is what separates great leaders from people who happen to be in charge.

Great leaders multiply themselves through their team. They make their people better. Their absence doesn't sink the company; their system does.

## The Leadership Implication

If you're working 60+ hours and frustrated that "nothing works without you," the problem isn't your team.

It's that you haven't delegated.

And the reason you haven't? Usually one of the reasons above. "I'm faster." "They're not ready." "I like doing it."

Those are all true. And they're all preventing you from leading.

**Real leadership is making your team better than you are.**

Not at everything. But at their domain. They become better at sales than you. Better at operations. Better at the specific work you've given them.

That's when scaling becomes possible.

## Your Next Move

1. **List your 20 most time-consuming tasks.** Not thinking about it. Actually write them down for a week.

2. **Categorize them.** Type 1 (only you), Type 2 (you oversee), Type 3 (delegate).

3. **Type 3s are your candidates for immediate delegation.** Pick one.

4. **Define the standard.** What does "done right" look like?

5. **Find the person.** Who has capacity and interest in growing here?

6. **Delegate with authority.** Give them responsibility, not just the task.

7. **Set checkpoints.** Weekly for the first month. Then biweekly.

8. **Support them.** When they struggle, help them improve. Don't just take it back.

Watch what happens.
