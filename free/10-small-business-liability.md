# The Small Business Liability You're Probably Ignoring

## The Silent Risk

A small business owner is sued.

A customer trips on a crack in her storefront. A team member claims discrimination. A contractor says they were never paid and sues for breach of contract.

The owner thinks: "I have insurance. They'll handle it."

But here's what happens:

1. Insurance company investigates.
2. They find the business *wasn't* properly set up legally.
3. They deny the claim.
4. The owner pays the legal bills and the settlement personally.

This happens to 1 in 5 small business owners. And by the time they realize the liability, it's too late.

---

## The Three Liabilities That Destroy Small Businesses

Most small business owners know they need insurance. What they don't know is that insurance doesn't protect you if your legal structure is wrong.

### Liability 1: Piercing the Corporate Veil

You set up an LLC to "protect yourself."

But you don't actually run it like a separate entity. You:

- Don't maintain separate bank accounts
- Mingle personal and business money
- Don't hold annual meetings (even on your own)
- Make personal guarantees on business contracts
- Sign contracts as "John Smith" instead of "John Smith, CEO of XYZ LLC"

A customer sues your business. They win. They go after *your* personal assets.

A lawyer argues: "The LLC is just a shell. He treated it like his personal checking account. We should pierce the veil and go after his house."

And they can. Because the corporate veil is pierced.

**The liability:** Unlimited personal liability. Everything you own is at risk.

**The fix:**

1. **Separate bank accounts.** Business money goes in a business account. Personal money stays personal.

2. **Minimal personal guarantees.** If a bank requires you to guarantee a business loan personally, fine. That's common. But don't do it for routine contracts.

3. **Treat the entity like a real company.** Not obsessively. But at least:
   - Sign contracts with your title (CEO, Owner, etc.)
   - Keep basic records
   - Don't put personal expenses through the business account

4. **Formality matters legally, not operationally.** You don't need monthly board meetings for a one-person LLC. But sign documents properly.

**Cost of fixing this:** About $200 in documents and a bit of discipline.

**Cost of not fixing this:** Your house, your car, your savings. Potentially millions.

### Liability 2: Employee Classification

You have 3 "contractors" doing work that looks a lot like employment.

They:

- Work 40 hours per week
- Use your equipment
- Report to you daily
- Follow your processes
- Work exclusively for you

But you classify them as independent contractors because it saves you payroll tax.

The IRS audits you. They reclassify them as employees.

Now you owe:

- Back payroll taxes (15.3% each for you and them)
- Penalties for misclassification
- Interest on everything
- Potentially wage-and-hour violations if you didn't pay overtime

**The liability:** $50k-$500k depending on how many people, how long they've worked for you, and your state's laws.

**The fix:**

Use the "ABC test" (varies by state, but the general concept):

**A:** The worker is free from control and direction.

**B:** The worker performs services outside the usual course of your business.

**C:** The worker is customarily engaged in an independently established business.

If all three aren't true, they're an employee.

Examples:

- **Contractor:** An accountant you hire once a year to do your taxes. They control their own process, do this for many clients, are an established business.

- **Employee:** Someone working 40 hours/week in your office on your core service. They're not independent. You control the work. It's your usual business.

**Cost of fixing this:** Reclassify people and set up payroll. Maybe $1,000-$3,000 one-time.

**Cost of not fixing this:** Tens of thousands in back taxes and penalties, plus lawsuits.

### Liability 3: Undisclosed Related-Party Transactions

You own the business. Your brother-in-law needs work. You hire him.

Fair wage? Not really. You pay him $40/hour for work that would cost $60/hour from a contractor.

You write it off as a business expense. It reduces your taxable income.

The IRS audits. They see:

- You paid a related party below-market wages
- You likely had a hidden arrangement
- You improperly reduced your taxable income

They reclassify the expense. You owe back taxes.

Or worse: they argue it's fraud. Now you're looking at criminal liability.

**The liability:** Back taxes, penalties, and potentially fraud charges.

**The fix:**

1. **If you hire family, document it.** Show that the wages are market-rate for the work.

2. **Get independent appraisals for big purchases** involving related parties. If you "sell" real estate to an entity you own, get an appraisal showing it's at fair market value.

3. **Disclose everything.** If there's a related-party transaction, put it in your tax return. Tell your accountant. Document the business purpose.

4. **When in doubt, ask.** Call your accountant before entering into related-party deals. "I want to hire my brother. Here's the work, here's what I'm proposing to pay. Is this okay?"

**Cost of fixing this:** Maybe $500 in appraisals and documentation per transaction.

**Cost of not fixing this:** Tens of thousands in back taxes, penalties, and potential fraud charges.

## The Liability Checklist

Go through this. Answer honestly.

**Corporate Structure:**

- [ ] Do you have a separate business bank account?
- [ ] Do you sign contracts with your business title (not just your name)?
- [ ] If you have an LLC or S-Corp, are you actually maintaining the structure?

**Employee Classification:**

- [ ] Do you have any "contractors" who work 30+ hours/week for you?
- [ ] Do they use your equipment?
- [ ] Do they report directly to you or follow your processes?
- [ ] Do they work exclusively for you?

If yes to all four: they should probably be employees.

**Related-Party Transactions:**

- [ ] Have you hired family members?
- [ ] Have you sold assets to your own entity?
- [ ] Have you paid yourself below-market rates or above-market rates?
- [ ] Have you borrowed money from the business personally, or vice versa?

If yes to any: have you documented that it's at fair market value?

## The Invisible Cost

Most small business owners don't get sued or audited. They're fine.

But the ones who *do* are shocked at how much it costs.

Not just legally. Operationally.

An IRS audit takes 100 hours of time (yours and your accountant's). A lawsuit takes 500 hours (discovery, depositions, testimony).

That's equivalent to months of lost business productivity.

All preventable with basic legal structure.

---

## Why Owners Avoid This

1. **It feels complex.** Setting up an LLC, separating accounts, documenting contracts—it *sounds* hard.

   Reality: It's not. An attorney can do it in 2-4 hours.

2. **They assume insurance covers it.** It doesn't. Insurance covers accidents. It doesn't cover legal liability from poor structure.

3. **"It won't happen to me."** Statistically, you might be right. But it only takes one lawsuit to ruin you.

4. **Cost avoidance.** "If I misclassify contractors, I save 15% in payroll taxes." True. You also risk 10x that amount in penalties.

## What To Do This Week

1. **Call an accountant.** (Not a tax prep service. An actual CPA or accountant.)

   "I want to make sure my business structure is right. I'm set up as [LLC/S-Corp/other]. Do I need to change anything?"

   Cost: $300-$500 for a consultation.

2. **Review your contractor agreements.** Anyone you classify as a contractor—do they meet the ABC test? If not, reclassify or get legal guidance.

3. **Review related-party transactions.** Any deals with family, other entities you own, etc.—are they documented at fair market value?

4. **Get the right insurance.** General liability, professional liability if applicable, workers' comp if you have employees. Not because it'll save you if you're sued, but because it covers the things insurance *should* cover.

## One More Thing: The Handbook

If you have employees, you need an employee handbook.

Not a 40-page corporate thing. Something simple that covers:

- At-will employment
- Harassment and discrimination policy
- Leave and time-off policies
- Expense reimbursement
- Code of conduct

Why? Because if someone claims they were treated unfairly, you can point to the handbook and show you had a clear policy.

If you have no handbook, you have no defense.

Cost to draft one: $500-$1,500 (attorney) or $0 (template from the internet, then have an attorney review it for $300).

Cost of defending yourself against a discrimination claim without one: $50k-$500k+.
