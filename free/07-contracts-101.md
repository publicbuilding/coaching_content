# Contracts 101: What You Can't Afford to Miss

## The Reality

Most business owners don't read their contracts.

They skim them. They see "looks standard" and sign. They hope nothing goes wrong.

Then something does. And they discover the contract says something very different from what they *thought* it said.

This section isn't about becoming a lawyer. It's about understanding the four sections of every contract that actually *matter*—and the five phrases that can destroy you if you don't catch them.

---

## The Four Sections That Matter

### Section 1: The Scope (What Are We Actually Doing?)

This is where miscommunications happen. One party thinks "support includes onboarding." The other thinks "support means bug fixes only."

**What to look for:**

- **What exactly are you delivering?** Be specific. Not "consulting services," but "50 hours of consulting focused on building your marketing strategy, including 4 working sessions and a 20-page strategic document."

- **What's *not* included?** This is actually more important. "Does not include: implementation, staff training, or ongoing support."

- **What does "complete" mean?** For software: does it need to pass testing? For services: who approves that the work is done?

- **What's the timeline?** Not just the deadline, but when do you start? Are there milestones?

**Why it matters:** 80% of contract disputes come down to scope misunderstanding. You think you're done. The client thinks you're not. Fight.

**Questions to ask:**

- "If we both read this in 6 months, would we have the same understanding of what was delivered?"
- "What's an example of something that's *not* included here?"
- "If I finish this on time but you're not happy, is that a problem?"

---

### Section 2: Payment (When and How Much?)

This is straightforward, but people rush through it.

**What to look for:**

- **How much?** (Obviously.)
- **When?** "Upon completion" is worse than "50% upfront, 50% upon completion."
- **How?** Check, ACH, invoice 30 days net? (Net 30 means they don't owe you for 30 days after invoice.)
- **What triggers payment?** Do they need to approve something first? Can they hold payment if they're unhappy?

**Why it matters:** Payment terms determine your cash flow. "Net 90" means you're funding their project. You might go broke waiting.

**Red flags:**

- "Payment upon satisfaction." (Vague. They define satisfaction. Bad for you.)
- "Net 60 or 90." (You're a bank.)
- No mention of late fees. (They can pay whenever; you have no recourse.)
- "Final payment upon successful deployment." (Ambiguous. What's "successful"?)

**Better language:**

- "50% upon signing, 50% upon delivery. All invoices due within 15 days."
- "Net 15. $X% fee for any invoice more than 30 days overdue."
- "Payment due within 5 business days of project completion. Completion is defined as [specific deliverable] passing [specific test]."

---

### Section 3: Liabilities and Warranties (What Happens If Something Goes Wrong?)

This is the section that scares people, so they avoid it. Mistake.

**What to look for:**

- **What are you guaranteeing?** Are you saying the software will never crash? That the strategy will work? That the product is defect-free?

- **What's *not* guaranteed?** This is actually the important part.

- **What happens if something goes wrong?** Do they get a refund? Can they sue? For how much?

- **Indemnification.** (This is lawyer-speak for "who pays if a third party sues?") If your software infringes a patent, do *you* pay for their legal defense, or do they?

**Why it matters:** One bad clause can make you liable for unlimited damages. Or it can shield you appropriately.

**Red flags:**

- "The contractor guarantees the work will be perfect." (Impossible to deliver. Don't promise it.)
- "Unlimited liability." (You could owe $10M for a $50k project.)
- "The contractor indemnifies the client against all claims." (You're paying their legal bills forever if anyone sues.)
- No mention of liability caps. (If something goes wrong, how much can they sue for?)

**Better language:**

- "The contractor warrants the work will be performed professionally and meet the specifications in Section 1. The contractor does not warrant error-free operation."
- "Each party's liability is capped at the total fees paid in this contract." (You can't owe more than you earned.)
- "Client is responsible for indemnifying contractor against claims arising from the client's use of deliverables outside the scope of this agreement." (If they misuse it, they pay.)

---

### Section 4: Termination (How Do We Get Out of This?)

Nobody reads this until they need to. Then it's too late.

**What to look for:**

- **Can either of us terminate early?** For cause? For convenience (just deciding not to do it)?
- **How much notice?** 30 days? Immediate?
- **What happens to money?** Do they owe you for work done? Do you refund them?
- **What happens to the work?** Do they get access to deliverables if they terminate?

**Why it matters:** If the relationship isn't working, you need an exit. If there's no clear exit, you're trapped.

**Red flags:**

- "Cannot be terminated. Binding for 3 years." (Trap. Avoid.)
- "Early termination results in loss of all fees paid." (Punitive. Unfair.)
- "Termination requires 6 months notice." (Who can actually plan 6 months ahead?)
- No mention of what happens to work in progress. (If they terminate, do they own it? Do you keep it?)

**Better language:**

- "Either party may terminate for cause with 10 days written notice if the other party materially breaches and doesn't cure within that period."
- "Either party may terminate for convenience with 30 days written notice. Client pays contractor for all work completed plus 50% of fees for the remainder of the contract period."
- "Upon termination, all deliverables completed to date remain the property of the client. All work in progress reverts to the contractor."

## The Five Phrases That Can Destroy You

### 1. "Reasonable Efforts"

"The contractor will make reasonable efforts to meet the deadline."

This is squishy. What's "reasonable"? You worked 40 hours? That seems reasonable. But the client thinks you should have worked 80.

**Better:** "The contractor will deliver by [specific date]. If the deadline is missed due to factors outside the contractor's control, the contractor will [specific remedy]."

### 2. "Best of Ability"

"The contractor will provide services to the best of their ability."

Same problem. What's your "ability"? Who judges?

**Better:** "The contractor will provide services meeting industry standards for [specific service]."

### 3. "As Soon As Possible" / "ASAP"

"The contractor will respond to issues ASAP."

ASAP to whom? Someone might think 2 hours. Someone else thinks 2 days.

**Better:** "The contractor will respond to critical issues within 4 business hours, major issues within 8 business hours, and minor issues within 2 business days."

### 4. "Standard Practice" / "Industry Standards"

"The contractor will follow industry standards for data security."

Which industry? Whose standards? HIPAA? SOC 2? Both? Neither?

**Better:** "The contractor will encrypt all data in transit and at rest, maintain encrypted backups, and conduct annual security audits per [specific framework]."

### 5. "At the Contractor's Discretion"

"Additional features will be included at the contractor's discretion."

This is a get-out-of-jail-free card for you, but it's dangerous. It means the client doesn't know what they're getting.

**Better:** "The contractor will include [specific features]. Additional features requested by the client can be added at $X per feature."

## Before You Sign: The Checklist

Go through every contract with this checklist:

1. **Scope:** Can I articulate exactly what I'm delivering?
2. **Payment:** Is the payment structure favorable? Am I funding them?
3. **Liability:** Am I liable for unlimited damages? Have I capped it?
4. **Indemnification:** Am I paying for their legal problems?
5. **Warranties:** Am I promising something impossible?
6. **Termination:** Can I get out if this doesn't work?
7. **Vague language:** Are there phrases I'd have to argue about in court?
8. **Timeline:** Is it realistic? Do I have flexibility?
9. **Exclusivity:** Am I locked into serving only them?
10. **IP ownership:** Who owns what I create?

If you can't answer these 10 questions clearly, don't sign.

## What a Lawyer Actually Costs

"But hiring a lawyer to review contracts is expensive!"

Yeah. It's also cheaper than getting sued.

A lawyer reviewing a contract: $500-$1,500 depending on length.

A lawsuit over a bad contract: $50,000-$500,000+ in legal fees, settlement, or judgment.

Do the math.

## The Psychology of Signing

Here's what happens: You've spent hours negotiating. You're tired. The contract is 10 pages. You think "it's standard." You sign.

This is when bad things happen.

**Force yourself to actually read it.** Not skim. Read. Slowly.

For every sentence that makes you even slightly uncomfortable, ask a question. Make the client change it or explain it.

If they won't change obviously problematic language, that's a signal about how they handle problems. A client who fights you on clear contract language will fight you on everything.

---

## The Real Goal

The contract isn't about having a lawsuit-proof agreement. It's about being on the same page.

If you both understand:

- What you're delivering
- When you're delivering it
- How much you're being paid
- What happens if something breaks
- How you both can get out

...you'll rarely need the contract to settle disputes.

It's only when you *don't* agree on those basics that the contract becomes relevant. And by then, it's too late.

## One More Thing: NDAs

If a client asks you to sign an NDA, read it carefully.

"Standard" NDAs often have vague language that makes you liable for things you can't control.

Red flags:

- "All information is confidential, including..." (with no definition of "information")
- "Confidentiality lasts forever"
- "Breach results in liquidated damages of $X" (automatic penalty)

**Better:** "Confidential information means [specific definition]. This obligation expires after 2 years. If you use public information, no breach occurs."
