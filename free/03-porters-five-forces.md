# Porter's Five Forces: A 5-Minute Refresher

## Why You Need This Framework

You're planning strategy. Your team is excited about a new market or product. But before you commit resources, you need to understand one thing: **Is this market actually attractive, or are we walking into a trap?**

Michael Porter's Five Forces framework answers that question. It's been used by executives for 40+ years because it works.

The basic idea: **Profitability isn't determined by your company alone.** It's determined by the *competitive structure* of your industry. Some industries are naturally profitable. Others trap you in a low-margin race to the bottom.

This framework helps you see which is which—and what to do about it.

---

## The Five Forces

Imagine your company at the center, pressured by five competitive forces:

```
                    Threat of
                  New Entrants
                        |
                        ↓
Bargaining Power      YOUR        Bargaining Power
of Suppliers ←———— COMPANY ————→ of Customers
                        ↑
                        |
                   Threat of
                Substitute Products
```

Each force creates pressure that squeezes profit margins. Strong forces = harder to be profitable. Weak forces = easier to win.

---

## Force #1: Threat of New Entrants

**The Question**: How easy is it for a new competitor to enter this market?

**Low Threat (Good for you):**
- High capital requirements (need $500M to build a factory)
- Strong brand loyalty (customers won't switch)
- Proprietary technology or patents
- Regulatory barriers (need licenses to operate)
- Network effects (value increases with users—think Facebook)
- High switching costs (customers locked in)

**High Threat (Bad for you):**
- Low barriers to entry (anyone can start)
- Commodity products (no switching costs)
- Abundant capital (startups can raise money easily)
- No moat (existing brands don't matter)

**Example**: 
- *High barriers*: Semiconductor manufacturing. New player needs $20B factory. Few competitors means stable margins.
- *Low barriers*: Content creation. Anyone with a laptop can start a blog. Thousands compete, margins stay thin.

---

## Force #2: Bargaining Power of Customers

**The Question**: How much power do your customers have to negotiate down your prices?

**Low Power (Good for you):**
- Few alternatives (you're the only option)
- High switching costs (customer invested in your system)
- Customer needs are critical (they *have* to buy from you)
- Fragmented customers (no single customer is huge)
- Product is cheap relative to customer's revenue

**High Power (Bad for you):**
- Many alternatives (customers can switch easily)
- Low switching costs
- Customer can buy from competitors
- Concentrated customers (one customer is 30% of revenue)
- You sell to price-conscious buyers

**Example**:
- *Low power customers*: Enterprise software vendors. Switching a CRM costs millions in training and data migration. Customers are locked in, can't easily price-shop.
- *High power customers*: Suppliers to Walmart. Walmart can demand price cuts or switch suppliers. One customer is 10%+ of revenue. Suppliers have thin margins.

---

## Force #3: Bargaining Power of Suppliers

**The Question**: How much power do your suppliers have to raise prices and squeeze your margin?

**Low Power (Good for you):**
- Many suppliers (you can play them against each other)
- Suppliers need your business more than you need them
- Easy to switch suppliers
- Abundant raw materials
- You buy in huge volume (giving you leverage)

**High Power (Bad for you):**
- Few suppliers (limited alternatives)
- Suppliers can integrate forward (start competing with you)
- Switching is costly
- Scarce materials
- You're a small customer relative to supplier's business

**Example**:
- *Low supplier power*: Agriculture. Thousands of farmers supply commodities. One farmer can't raise prices; many alternatives exist.
- *High supplier power*: Smartphone makers need rare components. If one supplier controls a chip, they can raise prices. Apple and Samsung both depend on TSMC.

---

## Force #4: Threat of Substitute Products

**The Question**: Can customers solve their problem another way?

This is the most underestimated force. Substitutes don't need to be identical—they just need to meet the customer's underlying need.

**Low Threat (Good for you):**
- No real substitutes exist
- Substitutes are expensive or inconvenient
- Customer strongly prefers your solution
- Switching to substitute costs money or effort

**High Threat (Bad for you):**
- Customers have multiple ways to solve the problem
- Substitutes are cheaper
- Substitutes are getting better (technology shift)
- Customers are indifferent between solutions

**Example**:
- *Low threat*: Insulin for diabetics. No good substitute. Patients must buy it.
- *High threat*: Taxi companies face Uber, Lyft, public transit, carpooling, remote work. Many substitutes. Taxi margins collapsed.

The biggest strategic mistakes come from ignoring substitute threats. Taxi companies weren't worried about each other; they didn't see Uber coming.

---

## Force #5: Industry Rivalry (Competitive Intensity)

**The Question**: How intensely do existing competitors fight?

**Low Rivalry (Good for you):**
- Few competitors
- Competitors differentiate (not racing on price)
- Industry is growing (enough pie for everyone)
- High switching costs (customers loyal to their choice)
- Competitors have different strategies
- High profit potential (no need to undercut)

**High Rivalry (Bad for you):**
- Many competitors
- Products are similar (race to the bottom on price)
- Slow industry growth (fighting for share)
- Low switching costs (customers shop around)
- Competitors are similar in size and strategy
- Low profit potential (everyone's squeezed)

**Example**:
- *Low rivalry*: Enterprise cloud computing. Azure, AWS, Google Cloud—few players, huge market, all profitable.
- *High rivalry*: Commodity chips. Thousands of makers, razor-thin margins, constant price wars.

## How to Use This: A Quick Analysis

**Step 1: Map each force** (Score 1-5, where 5 = high pressure)

For your industry/market:
- New entrants threat: ___
- Customer bargaining power: ___
- Supplier bargaining power: ___
- Substitute threat: ___
- Industry rivalry: ___

**Step 2: Look for the highest scores.** These are where profit goes.

**Step 3: Build strategy to counter them.**

## Real Example: Fast Casual Restaurants

```
Threat of New Entrants:       ⭐⭐⭐⭐ (High)
  - Anyone can open a restaurant
  - Capital is moderate
  - No legal barriers

Bargaining Power of Suppliers: ⭐⭐ (Low)
  - Food suppliers are abundant
  - Easy to switch

Bargaining Power of Customers: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ (Very High)
  - Dozens of options nearby
  - No switching costs
  - Price-sensitive
  - Easy to choose competitor

Threat of Substitutes:         ⭐⭐⭐⭐ (High)
  - Fast food, sit-down restaurants, grocery stores
  - Meal kit services, home delivery
  - Customers have many options

Industry Rivalry:              ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ (Very High)
  - Hundreds of chains
  - Constant price competition
  - Difficult to differentiate
```

**Verdict**: This industry is structurally *unattractive*. Margins are thin because customers have power, substitutes exist, and new competitors are easy to start.

**Implication**: You can't win on price. You win on differentiation (unique food, experience, brand) or efficiency (better unit economics than competitors). This is why chains like Chipotle and Shake Shack focus on brand and quality, not competing with McDonald's on price.

## Strategic Implications

Once you understand the forces, you can:

1. **Avoid bad markets.** Don't enter industries with all five forces stacked against you.

2. **Build defensible positions.** Invest in the areas that counteract the strongest forces. High customer bargaining power? Build switching costs. Many substitutes? Differentiate.

3. **Price intelligently.** High supplier power? You can't squeeze costs. High customer power? You can't raise prices without losing share.

4. **Plan long-term.** Forces shift over time. Yesterday's moat becomes tomorrow's commodity. Watch for change.

---

## Key Takeaways

- **The five forces determine industry profitability**, not your company's effort alone
- **Strong forces = thin margins, hard to win.** Weak forces = profitable, easier to be successful
- **Map the forces in your industry.** Score them. Understand where the pressure is greatest
- **Build strategy to counter the strongest forces**, not just to beat competitors
- **Revisit periodically.** Forces change. New entrants, new technologies, customer preferences shift

---

## One More Thing: The Trap

The biggest mistake? Using Five Forces to say "our industry is attractive" or "our industry is terrible," then doing nothing.

The real value is in the *specificity*. You might identify that:
- Threat of new entrants is high, BUT you can build a brand moat
- Customer bargaining power is high, BUT we can lock them in with service quality
- Substitutes are emerging, BUT we can differentiate faster than substitutes improve

Five Forces isn't destiny. It's a map. Once you see the terrain, you navigate it.
