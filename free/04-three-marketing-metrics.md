# The Only 3 Marketing Metrics That Matter

## The Metric Trap

You can measure anything in marketing. Website traffic, email open rates, social media engagement, ad impressions, conversion funnels, customer acquisition cost, lifetime value, retention rate, NPS, feature adoption, content performance...

50+ metrics. Every agency wants to report on them all.

Your CMO tells you the numbers are great. But revenue is flat.

**This is the metric trap.** You can look busy—measuring everything—while completely missing whether marketing is actually *working*.

The truth is brutal and simple: **most marketing metrics are vanity.** They make you feel good but don't tell you if money is being made.

---

## The Three Metrics That Matter

If you track only three things, pick these. Everything else is noise.

### Metric #1: Cost Per Qualified Lead (CPQL)

This is what you spend to get one real, interested prospect in your pipeline.

Notice: **qualified.** Not "someone visited the website." Not "someone opened an email." Someone who actually has a problem you solve and budget to fix it.

**How to calculate it:**

- Total marketing spend (ads, content, events, software, people) = $50,000/month
- Qualified leads generated = 100
- CPQL = $500/lead

**Why this matters:**

If you're spending $500 to get a lead and your average deal is $2,000, you're in trouble (3.3x markup on deal size).

If you're spending $500 to get a lead and your average deal is $50,000, you're golden (100x markup).

CPQL immediately tells you if your marketing math works. If it doesn't, something's broken at the source.

**The leadership question:** Is this trending up or down? If it's going *up* (costs rising, efficiency falling), you need to act now. If it's going *down*, you're getting better. That's the trend that matters.

---

### Metric #2: Sales Conversion Rate (Pipeline to Revenue)

This is the percentage of qualified leads your sales team actually closes.

**How to calculate it:**

- Qualified leads generated = 100
- Deals closed = 20
- Conversion rate = 20%

**Why this matters:**

This tells you if your marketing is actually *qualified,* or if it's just generating noise that your sales team has to filter.

A CMO might brag: "We generated 500 leads this month!"

Sales VP says: "Yeah, but 450 of them weren't ready to buy. Our *real* pipeline grew by 50."

If your conversion rate is low (below 15%), your marketing either:

1. **Doesn't understand the customer.** You're attracting the wrong people.
2. **Promises the wrong thing.** You're setting expectations that don't match the product.
3. **Attracts bargain hunters.** You're marketing on price instead of value.

If your conversion rate is high (25%+), your marketing is *really* good at attracting the right person at the right time.

**The leadership question:** Is conversion trending up or down? If up, your messaging is getting better. If down, you're drift.

---

### Metric #3: Customer Acquisition Cost vs. Customer Lifetime Value (CAC vs. LTV Ratio)

This is the ROI of marketing in its purest form.

**How to calculate it:**

- Total marketing spend = $100,000/month
- New customers acquired = 50
- CAC = $2,000/customer

- Average customer revenue/year = $12,000
- Average customer stays = 3 years
- LTV = $36,000
- LTV:CAC ratio = 18:1 (for every $1 spent, you get $18 in revenue)

**Why this matters:**

This is the only metric that actually connects marketing spend to *profit.*

A healthy SaaS company targets a 3:1 ratio (minimum) to 5:1 ratio (ideal).

B2B services might target 2:1 (because sales also costs money).

Below 2:1 and your unit economics are broken. You're losing money on every customer you acquire.

Above 5:1 and either:

1. You've got a world-class product (rare)
2. You've got a niche market (sustainable)
3. Your LTV calculation is optimistic (likely)

**The leadership question:** Is your ratio trending toward your target? If not, what needs to change—the cost to acquire, or the value customers actually deliver?

## Why Everything Else Doesn't Matter

**Website traffic:** Meaningless. You could get a million visits from people who'll never buy anything.

**Email open rates:** Feels good but doesn't mean you're making money. A 45% open rate on an email campaign that generates zero qualified leads is a failure.

**Social media engagement:** These metrics incentivize noise, not business impact. A viral post that generates zero leads is a waste of time.

**Ad impressions:** This is what ad networks *want* you to measure, because it makes them look good. But an impression to someone not interested in you is worthless.

**Website conversion rate (general):** This can be helpful internally (if you're A/B testing website elements), but it's not a strategic metric. You could have a 10% website conversion rate while generating zero real business outcomes.

**Cost per click, impressions, reach, etc.:** These are *inputs* to the system, not *outputs.* They're useful for optimization, but not for strategy.

## How to Implement These Three Metrics

### For a B2B Service Business

**CPQL:** Track total marketing spend ÷ qualified leads. Qualified = they filled out a form AND met with your team AND fit your ideal customer profile.

**Conversion:** Of qualified leads, what % sign a contract? Track this monthly.

**CAC:LTV:** Average project revenue × 2 (for repeat work and referrals) ÷ acquisition cost.

### For a SaaS Business

**CPQL:** Total marketing spend ÷ free trial signups that show intent (used the product, not just signed up).

**Conversion:** Free trials → paying customers. What's your conversion rate?

**CAC:LTV:** Annual subscription value × expected lifetime (in years) ÷ marketing cost per customer.

### For E-Commerce

**CPQL:** Total marketing spend ÷ visitors who added to cart (intent signal).

**Conversion:** Visitors to cart → customers.

**CAC:LTV:** Average order value × repeat purchase rate × average lifetime ÷ marketing cost.

## The Real Signal

Here's what these three metrics tell you:

1. **CPQL** = Are you reaching the right people cost-effectively?
2. **Conversion** = Are they actually interested in what you're selling?
3. **CAC:LTV** = Is this economically sustainable?

If all three are trending in your favor, your marketing is *working.*

If any one is breaking down, you've found your problem:

- CPQL up = Your messaging is getting expensive relative to supply. Your positioning might be too broad.
- Conversion down = Your leads don't actually match your sales process. Sales and marketing aren't aligned.
- CAC:LTV declining = You're acquiring cheaper customers (good) but they're staying shorter (bad). Product issue, not marketing issue.

## The Bonus: Why Your Agency Can't Tell You These Numbers

Most marketing agencies will resist this framework. They'll say:

- "These are hard to calculate"
- "The data isn't clean"
- "We need to measure more touchpoints"
- "Attribution is complicated"

What they mean is: "If we have to report on these, you'll fire us."

These numbers *are* harder to track than impressions. But they're not impossible. If your agency won't measure their output in actual business impact, that's a management failure on your part.

You should be able to answer these three questions in 15 minutes:

1. How much are we spending to get a real prospect?
2. What % of prospects are we closing?
3. Are we making a profit on the customers we're acquiring?

If you can't, your marketing system is broken.

---

## Moving from Vanity to Accountability

Implementing this framework means:

1. **Define "qualified lead"** — What actually counts as a qualified lead for your business? Not a guess. A real definition.

2. **Connect marketing to sales pipeline** — Marketing software and CRM should talk. You need to track from lead to deal in one system.

3. **Hold sales and marketing accountable to the same leads** — Sales team quality checks incoming leads. Marketing team tracks what sales actually pursues. Monthly alignment meeting.

4. **Calculate CAC and LTV honestly** — Not optimistically. Real numbers from real data.

5. **Report these monthly.** Not quarterly. Monthly. Watch the trend.

## The Strategic Implication

Once you're tracking these three metrics, something shifts in how you think about marketing.

You stop asking: "Did people see our ad?" (Vanity.)

You start asking: "Did this cost less to acquire the customer than the customer is worth?" (Reality.)

That's the difference between marketing as art and marketing as business.
